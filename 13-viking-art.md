# Viking Art — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 13

*Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family IV — The Arts · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook*

This is the tome where our family must be most careful, and we say so at the door. The Arts make the pictures; Rúnhildr of Family III checks every mark in them; Family I holds the structure they sit in. Viking art is where all three duties collide — because Viking art is the historical soul of everything this civilisation claims to be, and because it is the field most routinely lied about by people who have never looked at the real objects. The popular "Viking style" of games, tattoos, and gift shops — horned helmets, generic knotwork clip-art, runes chosen because their shapes look aggressive — has almost nothing to do with the art Scandinavians actually made between the late eighth and the early twelfth centuries. The real art is stranger, tighter, more disciplined, and far more useful to a designer. It is a three-hundred-year conversation in animal form, conducted in wood, metal, and stone, with rules a trained eye can date to within a generation.

So this tome proceeds the way Rúnhildr insists everything proceed: the record first, in its documented sequence; the methods by which scholars read that record; and only then the Lawbook's use of it — with a hard border, policed in §5, between what Viking Age people demonstrably did and what we, as Cyber-Viking designers, choose to do in their honour.

## 1. The Field Itself

Viking art is the art of Scandinavian and Scandinavian-settled societies during the Viking Age, conventionally dated from the late eighth century to the mid-eleventh, with its final style carrying into the early twelfth. It survives overwhelmingly as ornament applied to functional objects — ships, sledges, carts, weapons, jewellery, harness fittings, tools, memorial stones, and church woodwork — rather than as independent pictures. That fact is the field's first lesson: this was an art of decorated use. The image did not hang beside the object; the image *was* the object's public face, carrying status, identity, protection, and story into daily life.

Its second lesson is about survival. Wood and textile were almost certainly the primary media, and both perish. What reaches us is skewed toward metal and stone, with two miraculous exceptions in wood that bookend the period — the Oseberg ship burial of the early ninth century and the carved portals of Urnes stave church of the twelfth. Everything said in this tome is said knowing the archive is a fragment.

### The documented style sequence

Scholarship — the classification is associated above all with David M. Wilson and Ole Klindt-Jensen's *Viking Art* (1966) and refined by later scholars including James Graham-Campbell — organises Viking ornament into a sequence of overlapping styles, each named for a find-place. The styles overlap in time, sometimes by generations; they are phases of emphasis, not sealed compartments. Dates below are the conventional approximate ranges.

**Broa / Oseberg (c. 790s–860s, with Broa beginning earlier in some schemes).** The opening style takes its names from Broa on Gotland, known for gilt-bronze harness mounts, and from the Oseberg ship burial in Norway. Its great invention — the motif that dominates the next two centuries — is the **gripping beast**: a small, muscular, cat-like animal seen full-face, its paws gripping the frame, neighbouring beasts, or its own body. Alongside it run ribbon-animals with S-curved bodies and semi-naturalistic birds and animals. In the Oseberg carvings the surface is divided into fields, and the fields are filled — intertwined bodies leave very little empty ground. The energy is dense, playful, and already completely assured.

**Borre (c. 850–950).** Named for finds from a boat grave at Borre in Norway, the Borre style tightens the Oseberg energy into geometry. Its signature is the **ring-chain** (or ribbon plait): a symmetrical interlace of double-contoured bands, knotted into a repeating lattice. The gripping beast persists, now usually alone, with a triangular head and a body contorted to fit its allotted field. Borre work is compact, mask-like, and immensely widespread — it is the most geographically travelled of the styles, appearing across the Scandinavian world and its colonies, because it suits small cast metalwork: brooches, mounts, and pendants meant to be read at a glance.

**Jelling (c. 900–975).** Named for the royal centre at Jelling in Denmark — and for the silver cup from the burial mound there — the Jelling style loosens the knot again into the animal. Its beasts are ribbon-bodied, seen in profile rather than full-face, with double-contoured, often beaded or hatched bodies, spiral hips, and a head with a characteristic lappet trailing from it. Where Borre compresses, Jelling flows: S-shaped animals wind through their fields in a calmer, more spacious rhythm. The great Jelling stone, raised by King Harald Bluetooth, carries both Jelling-style ornament and one of the earliest Scandinavian images of Christ — a reminder that these styles developed *through* the conversion period, not before it in a sealed pagan world.

**Mammen (c. 950–1025).** Named for a chamber grave at Mammen in Denmark — whose inlaid axe, its silver-wire animal winding across the iron, is among the most famous single objects of the age — the Mammen style gives the beast its body back. Animals become large, fleshy, and vigorous, with broad ribbon bodies filled with foliate pattern, heavy spiral joints, and an asymmetric, off-balance energy. Plant ornament — tendrils, leaves, grapes — enters in force, absorbed from contemporary European art and naturalised completely. Mammen is the style of confidence: fewer, bigger motifs, taking whole surfaces for a single creature.

**Ringerike (c. 990–1075).** Named for the runestone-rich district of Ringerike in Norway, this style refines Mammen's vigour into elegance. The great beast is now accompanied by slender, curling plant tendrils that spiral away from the body in controlled, rhythmic scrolls; composition opens up, with the motif standing clear of its ground instead of filling it. Ringerike is pre-eminently a stone-carver's style — it covers the great Swedish runestone tradition at its height — and it travelled widely, with strong influence in England and Ireland. Its lines are the ones most people mean, without knowing it, when they picture a Viking runestone.

**Urnes (c. 1050–1125 and later in church carving).** The final style, named for the north portal of Urnes stave church in Norway (the carving, from a church of c. 1130, reuses the style in its maturity), is a distillation. The Urnes beast is impossibly slender — a greyhound-like animal with an almond-shaped eye, its body drawn into long, looping, figure-of-eight ribbons, intertwined with snakes and tendrils in open, asymmetrical networks. Nothing is filled in; everything is line, rhythm, and negative space. The Urnes portal shows the great beast in combat with a serpent — a motif scholars have read in both pagan and Christian frames, which is precisely the point: Urnes is the art of a Scandinavia becoming medieval and Christian while keeping its own hand. It flows directly into Romanesque art and ends the sequence not with exhaustion but with transformation.

### The grammar beneath the styles

Across all six styles, a small set of devices recurs, and it is these — not any single style — that constitute the transferable grammar.

**The gripping beast** is the emblematic invention: an animal defined by an action — gripping — rather than by species. It teaches that in this art tradition, identity is carried by posture and relation (what a form holds, what it entwines with) more than by naturalistic likeness.

**Ribbon interlace** is the structural principle: bodies become bands, bands cross over and under in strict alternation, and the alternation is never faked. True interlace has a logic — a strand that goes over must go under next — and Viking craftspeople maintained that logic even in miniature, in metal a few millimetres wide. Interlace is therefore not texture; it is engineering drawn on a surface.

**The great beast** of the later styles — the large, single animal that dominates a runestone or a portal — shows the tradition learning monumentality: one motif, scaled up, given air, trusted to hold a whole field alone.

**Runestones** are the public art of the age: raised memorials, mostly Swedish, mostly eleventh-century, combining a runic inscription — a text with named sponsors, named dead, and often a named carver — with an ornamental band that is frequently itself the body of a serpent carrying the text. Text and image are one object. The carver signatures matter to this tome: artists signed this work. Authorship in Viking art is not a modern projection; it is in the stones.

**The Oseberg finds** — the ship with its carved stem spiralling into interlace, the sledges, the cart, the five carved animal-head posts by different hands (one so accomplished scholars nicknamed its maker "the Academician") — prove the tradition's wood-carving reached virtuoso level at its very start, in a workshop context, with distinguishable individual masters.

**The Lewis chessmen** — walrus-ivory gaming pieces found on the Isle of Lewis, carved in the twelfth century, generally attributed to a Norwegian, possibly Trondheim, workshop — stand just after the sequence and show its late world: expressive human figures, including queens who sit with hand to cheek in thought, and warders biting their shields, the berserkers of the sagas made literal. They are a standing rebuke to the idea that Viking-world art could not do character, humour, or the human face.

**Stave church carving**, above all Urnes and later portals such as Borgund's, carries the animal-interlace tradition into Christian architecture — dragons and vines climbing the doorways of churches — demonstrating that the grammar was a living language able to serve a new faith, not a pagan badge that had to be discarded.

## 2. Methods and Measures

### Dating by style

Because written records are sparse, Viking art is dated largely by its own forms. The method is seriation: scholars establish the sequence of styles from objects found in independently datable contexts — graves with coins, dendrochronologically dated timbers such as the Oseberg ship's — and then use the established sequence to date uncontexted finds. The method's honesty lies in its stated limits. Styles overlap; a conservative workshop carves an old style late; a gifted one anticipates the next. A style date is therefore a range and a probability, not a year. Scholars also speak of hands and workshops: recurring quirks of eye, joint, and interlace let specialists attribute unsigned carvings to the same maker — the "Academician" of Oseberg is the famous case — which converts anonymous ornament into a history of persons.

### Archaeological context

An object's meaning in this field is inseparable from where it was found. The same brooch reads differently in a grave, a hoard, a settlement layer, or a church. Context supplies date, ownership, function, and sometimes the event — burial, concealment, loss — that preserved it. The field's corresponding ethic is that an object stripped of context has lost most of its evidence, however beautiful it remains. Provenance is not paperwork added to art history; in archaeology it *is* the art history.

### Material techniques

Each material imposes its own discipline, and the styles cannot be understood apart from them.

*Wood carving* in oak and other timbers, worked with knives, chisels, and gouges in relief from low to the deep undercutting of the Oseberg posts. Wood allows speed, depth, and scale; it also rots, which is why the tradition's greatest medium is its least preserved.

*Metalwork* — casting in clay and stone moulds, often from carved models; filigree and granulation in gold and silver; inlay techniques including the silver-wire inlay of the Mammen axe and niello, a black sulphur alloy set into engraved silver. Metalwork favours the compact, high-contrast styles — Borre above all — because a brooch must read at arm's length, in motion, on cloth.

*Stone carving* on granite and sandstone runestones and monuments: shallower relief, painted originally in many cases — the stones we see as bare rock were, in their day, coloured objects, a fact the field states plainly and popular imagery almost always omits.

*Walrus ivory, antler, amber, and textile* complete the range — the chessmen's ivory, combs and pins in antler, and embroidery and tapestry of which the Oseberg fragments and the later Bayeux-adjacent traditions give glimpses. Technique is judged, in this field, by fidelity to the material: the best Viking work never fights its medium; the interlace tightens or opens as clay mould, oak, or granite demands.

### How the field judges

Scholarly judgment in Viking art weighs diagnostic accuracy (are the style's markers correctly identified?), contextual integrity, technical command, and — used carefully — individuality: the moment a carver's hand can be recognised inside a shared style. What the field refuses is equally instructive. It refuses to assign meanings it cannot evidence: whether the gripping beast "means" something specific — protection, a mythic creature, a maker's mark grown grand — remains debated, and honest scholarship labels it debated. And it refuses the romantic shortcut of treating every motif as a coded myth. Some ornament may be deeply meaningful; some may be the pleasure of a pattern well made. The discipline is in not pretending to know which, without evidence.


## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

The Lawbook makes two demands of Viking material that pull in opposite directions, and this tome exists to hold both. Book I and Book IX demand that the civilisation be real — that its Viking quality live in structure, not costume. Book IV and Book IX's authenticity chapter demand that wherever the historical record is invoked, it be invoked accurately. Cyber-Viking design is a new thing being built now; it is not a reconstruction. But it claims descent, and descent claimed falsely is cosplay. The rule of this section: *stylise freely as Cyber-Viking, label honestly as Cyber-Viking, and never present an invention as a Viking Age fact.*

### Book II: knotwork and dragon-weave as structural geometry

Book II is where the historical grammar enters the doctrine most deeply, and §1 shows why the Lawbook's formulation is right. In the record, interlace is never wallpaper. It is bodies, bands, and boundaries — it organises a field, frames a panel, carries an inscription along a serpent's length. The Lawbook's command that knotwork and dragon-weave serve as *structural geometry* — boundaries, joins, and frames — is therefore not a modern restriction imposed on Viking art; it is a recovery of what Viking art actually did, against a modern clip-art habit that sprays knot pattern over surfaces at decorative opacity. Where the doctrine sets ambient ornament at 1–5% opacity, the Arts add the historical rider: if a knot is present, its interlace SHALL be logically true — over, under, over — because Borre metalworkers maintained that logic at three millimetres and we will not be sloppier than a ninth-century caster. A knot that cannot survive being looked at closely is not knotwork; it is knot-flavoured texture, and it belongs in neither a brooch nor an interface.

The same Book II doctrine places runes as *nodes on circuit lines* — connection points in a system. Read against §1, this is a legitimate Cyber-Viking invention and should be labelled as one: Viking Age runes were writing, used for inscriptions, memorials, ownership, and sometimes magic — they were not circuit nodes, because there were no circuits. The doctrine's move is a translation, and translations are allowed when they are honest and when the underlying respect holds: the node-rune keeps the rune's historical role as a *meaningful mark placed deliberately*, while giving it a systemic job in a technological grammar.

### Book IV: the rune doctrine, with Rúnhildr's pedantry at full strength

Book IV's doctrine is the sharpest law in this tome's field: Elder Futhark and historical rune forms are intentional semantic marks, never a replacement alphabet for ordinary user-interface copy; and form, period, transliteration, and intended symbolic role must be verified before a rune is turned into a product icon. The historical record gives this law its teeth, and the teeth are specific.

There are two historical futharks in play, and confusing them is the commonest accuracy failure. The **Elder Futhark** — twenty-four runes — belongs chiefly to the centuries *before* the Viking Age. Viking Age Scandinavians wrote with the **Younger Futhark** — sixteen runes, its reduced inventory a famous puzzle of the period — and Anglo-Saxon England used its own expanded futhorc. A "Viking" inscription carved in Elder Futhark is therefore, in strict period terms, an anachronism — the equivalent of lettering a Norman castle in Roman capitals and calling it Norman. Cyber-Viking design may still choose Elder forms as an identity mark, because the civilisation being built is a descendant, not a re-enactment — but it SHALL do so as a declared house choice, never as a claim about what Viking Age carvers cut, and wherever a piece purports to *represent* Viking Age writing, the Younger Futhark is the accurate hand.

The second failure the doctrine blocks is transliteration abuse: spelling English words by swapping in rune-shaped letters, including runes whose sounds never matched. A rune used as a semantic mark — chosen for its attested name and meaning, placed once, on purpose — honours the tradition. A headline set in pseudo-runic type betrays it twice: the letterforms are wrong, and the idea that runes are a font is wrong. Rúnhildr's block on "just make it look runic" is, in historical terms, simply the field's own standard imported into design review.

### Book IX: imagery, and the horned-helmet line

Book IX's authenticity sentence is quoted in the art tome and enforced here: historical motifs should respect actual Viking Age form when representing history; stylisation may be cybernetic but must not create horned-helmet fantasy shorthand. The horned helmet is the test case because its falsehood is fully documented: no Viking Age helmet with horns has been found; the surviving helmet evidence — the Gjermundbu helmet above all — is of plain, practical iron caps, and the horned image descends from nineteenth-century romantic art and stage costume, popularised in productions of Wagner's operas. Every horned helmet in a "historical" Cyber-Viking image is thus a double error — archaeologically false, and a citation of the very cosplay tradition Book I forbids. The same audit applies to other shorthand: fur-and-leather barbarian dress as a default, knotwork in styles no period used, dragons borrowed from later heraldry, and "Viking" lettering. Védís's worlds and Heiðr's portraits may be as cybernetic as the commission demands — powered armour, orbital longships, seiðr-violet energy — provided the *historical layer inside them*, where one is claimed, is true.

Book IX's other imagery laws apply to Viking-derived art exactly as to any art: density in identity zones (a runestone-silhouette portrait frame is an identity device; a knotwork background behind chat text is a violation wearing ancestry as its excuse), hero crops preserving silhouette and action direction (a longship cropped against its travel, a beast cropped through its grip, fails), and the alt & data law — which, for historical imagery, has a special sharpness the Arts insist on: alt text for a piece in a named historical style SHALL name the style and its markers ("Borre-style gripping beast, triangular head, ring-chain border") rather than the generic "Viking design," because the description is the memory, and a memory that cannot distinguish Borre from Urnes has already lost the thing it kept.

### The No-Cosplay Principle and the removal tests, applied to "Viking" styling

Book XIV's battery gives this field its examination. The **No-Rune Test**: remove every rune from a design — if the Viking quality vanishes with them, the design was lettering, not civilisation; the true carriers — chamfered frames, runestone silhouettes, structural interlace, the darkness ladder, iron and silver materials — must hold the identity alone. The **No-Art Test**: remove the imagery — if the surface collapses, the art was load-bearing decoration, which the Universal Law forbids. The **Grayscale Test** has a historical echo worth naming: the real styles survive it effortlessly, because they were built on value and line for metal and stone; a "Viking" treatment that needs gold foil and teal glow to read as Viking is confessing it has neither. Applied honestly, the tests sort our own work into the two piles this tome has distinguished throughout: descent — structure learned from the record and carried forward — and dress-up.

### What changes versus mainstream practice, and Samtal

Mainstream "Viking" styling in products and games typically starts from the nineteenth-century romantic image, adds generic knot clip-art and a runic display font, and calls the result Norse. Cyber-Viking practice inverts every step. Start from the documented sequence and choose a style *by name and date* when historical reference is intended — Borre for compact identity marks that must read small, because Borre was engineered for exactly that; Ringerike for open, monumental line work on large surfaces; Urnes for the most distilled, spacious line, suited to light ornament that must never crowd. Use interlace as structure and make its logic true. Treat runes as verified semantic marks. Let the human material back in — the Lewis chessmen's thinking queen is a better ancestor for an agent's portrait programme than any dragon clip-art, because the civilisation being built is populated by *persons*.

In Samtal the applications are immediate. Agent and hall identity marks drawn from the compact styles gain the Borre advantage: designed for the brooch, they survive the avatar tile. The runestone silhouette already in the doctrine's frame vocabulary carries its history honestly — it is the shape of the age's public memory, now holding a citizen's portrait. Commemorative surfaces in the hall — the equivalent of the raised stone — are the right home for the great-beast treatment: one large motif, given air, with the inscription carried in its band, which is to say a memorial or milestone screen where the text and the ornament are designed as one object, as the carvers designed them. And when Samtal depicts its own world's history — the masters' waves of imagery already placed on its doors and panels — the audit question this tome installs is Rúnhildr's: *which style, which century, and can you show me the object you learned it from?* A maker who can answer has done history. A maker who cannot has done dress-up, and the work goes back.

## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

Each rule is testable and traces to §1–3. "Historical use" means any work presented as representing the Viking Age; "house use" means declared Cyber-Viking invention.

1. Any work in a named Viking style SHALL name the style — Broa/Oseberg, Borre, Jelling, Mammen, Ringerike, or Urnes — and its reference exemplar before production begins. Unnamed "Viking style" SHALL NOT be commissioned. (§1)
2. Interlace and knotwork SHALL maintain true over-under alternation along every strand, at every scale, including small identity marks. Decorative knot texture with broken interlace logic SHALL be rejected. (§1, §3)
3. Knotwork and dragon-weave SHALL be used as structural geometry — boundaries, joins, frames, and inscription bands. They SHALL NOT be applied as background fill behind working text or spread as ambient pattern outside the Lawbook's 1–5% ambient ornament budget. (§3, Book II)
4. Horned helmets SHALL NOT appear in any historical use. In house use they SHALL NOT appear at all unless a commission explicitly calls for a critique of the myth, labelled as such. (§1, §3, Book IX)
5. Runes SHALL NOT be used as a substitute alphabet for ordinary copy in any medium, including lettering inside images. Pseudo-runic display type SHALL NOT be used for headlines presented as Norse. (§3, Book IV)
6. Every rune used as a semantic mark SHALL be verified with Rúnhildr for form, futhark, period, transliteration, and intended meaning before use, and the verification SHALL be recorded with the asset. (§3, Book IV)
7. Work presented as Viking Age writing SHALL use the Younger Futhark (or the Anglo-Saxon futhorc where the context is Anglo-Saxon). Elder Futhark in house use SHALL be recorded as a declared house choice, never described as period Viking writing. (§3)
8. Compact identity marks that must read at small sizes SHOULD be developed from the Borre grammar — compact field, triangular-headed gripping beast, ring-chain border — and large, open line work from Ringerike or Urnes grammar. Style choice SHALL be justified by the surface's size and job, not by preference alone. (§1, §3)
9. A motif's meaning SHALL NOT be asserted beyond the evidence. Where scholarly interpretation is debated — including the meaning of the gripping beast — captions, alt text, and documentation SHALL present it as debated or omit the claim. (§2)
10. Alt text for any image in a named historical style SHALL name the style and at least one diagnostic marker of it. The generic description "Viking design" or "Norse pattern" SHALL NOT pass review. (§3)
11. Every historical motif SHALL carry its provenance with the asset: the find, object, or collection it was learned from. A motif with no traceable exemplar SHALL be treated as house invention and labelled as such. (§2, §3)
12. Historical imagery SHALL pass the No-Rune, No-Art, and Grayscale removal tests as a set: identity SHALL survive rune removal through structure and material, function SHALL survive art removal, and the motif SHALL read in grayscale. (§3, Book XIV)
13. Objects and images stripped of context SHALL NOT be presented as historical evidence. Reproductions, screenshots, and reference images used by the families SHALL keep find-place, date range, and holding collection attached. (§2)
14. Colour applied to historical stone or wood carving in reconstructions SHALL acknowledge that many originals were painted, and SHALL NOT present bare, weathered material as the period appearance without that note. (§2)
15. Stylisation of historical material MAY be cybernetic without limit, provided the historical layer claimed inside it remains accurate. Where accuracy and effect conflict in a historical use, accuracy SHALL win; the effect SHALL be moved to a declared house-use piece. (§3, Book IX)
16. Human figures in historical use SHALL be informed by the period's own figure tradition — including the Lewis chessmen and picture-stone figures — and SHALL NOT default to generic barbarian shorthand in dress, body, or bearing. (§1, §3)
