Between us stands this field's oldest lesson: a model is not a mannequin with a pulse, and a photograph of a person is not decoration. ## 1. The Field Itself

### From dress forms to the living model

Before models there were substitutes: fashion dolls sent between courts, dress forms, sketches and swatches. The decisive break is conventionally credited to Charles Frederick Worth, who founded the House of Worth at 7 rue de la Paix in Paris in 1858. Worth presented seasonal collections of his own designs, sewed his label into his garments, and — crucially — showed them on live models instead of static figures, so clients could see how cloth draped and moved on a real body. His wife, Marie Vernet Worth, is widely regarded as his first house model. That move created the founding transaction, unchanged in essence: a living person's presence, carriage, and movement are lent to a garment or an idea, so someone not in the room can imagine it truthfully.

Houses kept their own *mannequins* presenting to small seated audiences; photography then split the profession. As *Harper's Bazaar* (founded 1867) and *Vogue* (founded 1892) moved from illustration to photography, a second modeling appeared: posing not for a room but for a lens, at scale — producing, in a frozen fraction of a second, a line and expression that still speak when flattened, cropped, and printed.

The agency system industrialised that ability. Ford Models, founded in New York in 1946 by Eileen and Gerard W. Ford, became the archetype: scouting, developing, booking, and negotiating for models as a business. Wilhelmina Models followed in 1967, founded by model Wilhelmina Cooper; Elite Model Management was founded in Paris in 1972. Agencies in the four capitals formed a seasonal circuit; a “mother agency” discovers and develops a model, then places her with partners abroad. In many markets she has historically worked as an independent contractor — a status debated in labour contexts, because agencies and clients control schedule, appearance, and pay while the model carries much of the risk.

### The named kinds of work

**Editorial modeling** is work for magazine stories and features — the spreads of *Vogue*, *Harper's Bazaar*, *Elle*, and their digital successors. It is narrative: a character sustained across a sequence. It has traditionally paid less than advertising while conferring prestige, and has historically imposed the narrowest casting. Its images are judged less on selling a garment than on creating a world a reader wants to enter.

**Commercial modeling** sells, explains, or reassures: campaigns, catalogues and e-commerce, lookbooks, packaging. Its casting has always been broader — more ages, body types, and faces — because its figures must be recognisable to the customers addressed. Its gift is legibility and trust: a smile that reads at thumbnail size, a posture that says the coat is warm.

**Runway modeling** is Worth's salon living on: a defined path, in sequence, in a house's garments, before buyers, press, and cameras. It demands fit compatibility with the designer's samples, but its core skill is kinetic — a rehearsed walk at the show's tempo, clean turns, management of difficult shoes and trains, and the discipline to remain part of a line of looks rather than a solo performance. Fashion weeks in New York, London, Milan, and Paris remain its most visible ritual, scheduled by the CFDA, the British Fashion Council, Italy's Camera Nazionale della Moda Italiana, and France's Fédération de la Haute Couture et de la Mode.

**Fit modeling** is the least glamorous and, to Eldríðr's eye, the most materially honest. A fit model is hired because her or his measurements consistently represent a brand's target size; garments are draped, pinned, and corrected on that living body, and the fit model gives feedback no dress form can — where a sleeve pulls, whether she can sit or reach. Fit modeling makes explicit what all modeling is: knowledge in a body, earned by wearing the thing.


### The photographic collaboration and the craft of posing

No fashion image is made by a model alone. The classic sitting is a collaboration of at least four crafts. The **photographer** owns light, lens, framing, and exposure, and usually directs pose and expression. The **stylist** owns the garment's presentation: selection, fit on the day, pinning out of sight, continuity. Hair and make-up artists own much of what a viewer attributes to “the model's face.” The **model** owns what none of them can supply: the body's line in the garment, the timing of a turn toward light, hands and gaze, and an expression range that moves on direction, in seconds — hauteur, warmth, melancholy, mischief, blankness — without caricature. Casting directors, art directors, and retouchers complete the chain; the retoucher's place becomes ethically decisive below.

Posing is craft, not accident. Its vocabulary descends from painting and sculpture — contrapposto through nineteenth-century portraiture — and can be named plainly. **Body line** is the continuous curve or angle from head through spine to foot; a good pose makes one decisive line and lets the garment hang from it. **Weight placement** tells whether a figure stands, leans, advances, or rests. **Hands** betray tension first, and earn their keep when they do a job: hold, fasten, carry, rest, gesture. **Gaze** sets the social contract: into the lens for direct address, past it for editorial distance, down or away for thought. **Negative space** — gaps between arm and torso, jaw and shoulder — decides whether a figure reads sculptural or flat. For stills, professionals work in slow micro-movement, offering a river of near-poses rather than one statue repeated. Runway posing is the same craft in time: the walk serves the garment's motion at the show's pace, with a pause and turn composed for the cameras — and expression restrained, because the show's fiction is collective.

### Canonical figures and what they prove

Certain names are safe ground, and instructive. Dovima, in Avedon's 1955 *Dovima with Elephants* for *Harper's Bazaar*, embodied the statuesque 1950s ideal — and Avedon's insistence on movement announced that fashion photography was leaving the mannequin behind. Jean Shrimpton, photographed extensively by David Bailey, carried 1960s fashion youthward and Londonward. Twiggy, discovered in 1966, made an androgynous, wide-eyed thinness a defining look — recorded as history, and returned to critically below, because an era's ideal is also an era's instruction to real bodies. Beverly Johnson's August 1974 cover was the first by a Black model on American *Vogue*; Donyale Luna had earlier broken barriers in British *Vogue* and *Harper's Bazaar*. Lindbergh's January 1990 British *Vogue* cover grouped Naomi Campbell, Linda Evangelista, Tatiana Patitz, Christy Turlington, and Cindy Crawford; Claudia Schiffer belongs to the same supermodel cohort. Their significance is not glamour. It is **presence as a durable asset**: identity surviving every photographer, decade, and crop — exactly what Book VIII demands of an agent portrait.

### Standards, sample sizes, and the documented problem

Here admiration stops and accounting begins, because the field's best-documented fact is the harm built into one working tool: the **sample size**. Designers produce collections first as single samples in one small size, and casting historically bent toward the bodies that fit them. The sample stopped being a production convenience and became a bodily standard enforced on very young workers — the “size zero” debate (US 0, UK 4) named its extreme.

The responses on record are specific, and this tome cites only ones it is certain of. In September 2006, Madrid's Pasarela Cibeles, with the city's regional government, turned away models with a body mass index below 18 — the first runway rule of its kind. Milan followed with an industry code in late 2006, and an Italian charter in February 2007 required health certificates and barred girls under 16 from Italian catwalks. Israel's 2012 Limitation of Weight in the Modeling Industry Act — the “Photoshop Act” — set a minimum BMI in advertising and required disclosure when an image was digitally altered to make a model appear thinner. France went furthest: its January 2016 health-system modernisation law required models to provide a medical certificate attesting overall health, weight considered by the doctor among other factors rather than by a single fixed BMI cut-off in the final text; and a decree of 5 May 2017, in force 1 October 2017, required commercial photographs whose body appearance had been thinned or thickened to be labelled *photographie retouchée* — with fines up to €37,500 for an unlabelled photograph, and up to €75,000 and imprisonment for employing a model in breach of the certificate rule; the labelling applies to commercial use, not editorial images. Norway extended the principle to the feed: a Marketing Control Act amendment in force 1 July 2022 requires advertisers and paid influencers to label advertising images in which a body's shape, size, or skin has been retouched, using a standardised mark. Getty Images announced in 2017 that it would no longer accept submissions retouched to make a model's body thinner or larger. The direction converges: the body in the image is a matter of public honesty, not only private taste.

Two further problems belong in any established account. First, **youth**: age floors — 16 in several codes, with chaperone and hours protections promoted by the CFDA and the British Fashion Council — exist because recruitment repeatedly reached ages when a worker can least negotiate. Second, **narrowness beyond size**: twentieth-century casting was overwhelmingly white, young, and able-bodied. The last decade's widening — curve boards, older beauty models, models with visible disabilities, Ashley Graham's 2016 *Sports Illustrated* Swimsuit cover — is genuine but uneven, and critics note campaign diversity can be seasonal rather than structural. Heiðr's judgment: a hall casting only one kind of face has failed to look at the world.


## 2. Methods and Measures

### Casting

Work begins at a **casting**. A casting director, client, or agency assembles candidates against a brief — the garment's needs, the audience, the other figures already cast, the sizes actually available — and models are seen briefly and judged quickly. The in-person form, traditionally a go-see, is deliberately unglamorous: form-fitting clothes, minimal make-up, hair wearable up or down, a short walk, a few still poses, and conversation enough to establish reliability. What is measured is not only appearance: proportion in motion, skin and hair under plain light, responsiveness to direction (“chin down, look left, softer”), punctuality, and temperament under waiting — a shoot day is mostly waiting.

A serious casting also measures fit against reality. Measurements are recorded — height; bust, waist, hips for women; chest, waist, inseam for men; size; shoe; hair and eye colour — and must be current and true: a model whose card says one thing and whose body says another wastes a fitting and breaks trust. They are rechecked at fittings, and a client needing a different size casts a different model rather than demanding this model become one. §4 makes that our rule without exception.

### Comp card, book, and digitals

Three artefacts carry a model between castings, each a small lesson in information design.

The **composite card — comp card or zed card** — is the leave-behind summary: one strong headshot; a small selection showing range (full body, editorial, commercial smile, beauty close-up); name, agency contact, and measurements. It succeeds by compression — identity, range, and hireability in seconds, without exaggeration. A card that misrepresents fails: the casting it wins will be lost at the fitting.

The **book, or portfolio**, is the longer argument: the best test and published images, ordered to show range without contradiction. New models build it through **test shoots** — unpaid or cost-shared collaborations where the whole team works for book images, planned like a real job in miniature and edited afterwards without sentimentality.

**Digitals** — historically Polaroids — are the anti-portfolio: recent, minimally retouched, simply lit photographs showing the model as she or he is today. Agency guidance is consistent: soft even light, neutral background, little or no make-up, hair natural and also pulled back, form-fitting simple clothing, covering full-body front, side, and three-quarter views, waist-up, and close-up face, smiling and unsmiling. Their ethic is this tome's ethic: **no filters, no body reshaping, no beautifying retouch**. Digitals exist so a decision-maker is never deceived about the material being hired; §3 gives our families their equivalent.

### On set: posing, light, and lens

A shoot day runs on a call sheet, a fitting, hair and make-up, then a rhythm of setups: one lighting arrangement serving a run of looks before the crew relights. Within each setup, the model works a pose like a phrase — body line, face to the light, hands last — then varies it in small increments. Photographers shoot through the variation; the truest frame is often a transition.

Working with **light** is bodily: turn face and torso into the key light or edge away for shadow, hold a sculpturally right but awkward position as long as the setup requires, then reproduce it after a break. Working with the **lens** is equally concrete: a short lens close up enlarges the nearest features; longer portrait lenses compress and flatter, but demand smaller, more precise movement. Cropping is negotiated in the body — a model who knows the frame is waist-up spends her expression differently from one whose feet, and the client's shoes, are in shot. Hands near the face are angled edge-on, never splayed flat toward the lens. None of this is a mystery of beauty; it is geometry, learnable and reviewable — which is why §4 can state it as tests. Judgment is layered — photographer on the frame, stylist on straps and creases, client on the monitor against the brief — and final judgment comes later, at the edit, for the frame where line, expression, garment, and light agree.

### Runway method, and expression as the measure

A runway show is rehearsed theatre with no second take. Models are fitted and assigned looks in running order; a choreographer sets the route — out, pause, turn, return — the pace against the music, and the spacing. Difficult garments are walked in the actual space at rehearsal; backstage runs on assigned dressers and quick-change choreography timed like a pit stop. Excellence is absolute: no missed cue, no garment mishandled, every look photographed cleanly at the runway's end; when a model falls, the admired response is recovery — rise, secure the garment, finish the walk.

Across all of it, **expression range** is the truest measure of value — neutral, smiling, laughing, severe, distant, intimate, each produced and left behind on request. A figure that can only do one expression has not been cast. It has been repeated.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

The Universal Law binds first: **every themed element must have a measurable job; when theme competes with task, reduce ornament, glow, texture, motion, or symbolic density before touching information architecture; the work must remain coherent in grayscale, at 125–200% text scaling, and with effects disabled.** A figure is a themed element. Its job must be statable in one sentence — *this portrait tells a newcomer whose hall this is; this hero carries the page's action direction* — or it is screenshot bias wearing a beautiful face, and it comes out.

### Book IX: portrait direction, adult design, the honest crop

Book IX — Imagery, Illustration & 3D — is this tome's home book; its laws are Subject, Composition, Atmosphere, Authenticity, Cropping, and Integration. Its target that **hero crops preserve subject silhouette and action direction** translates directly: never crop a figure so the body line is amputated mid-gesture, a walk's direction runs into the frame edge, or a garment's defining length — train, hem, fall of a cloak — is cut away while decorative background is kept. If the crop hides how the cloth is constructed or where it ends, it lies about the garment, however flattering it is to the face. Crop in this order: identity, garment truth, atmosphere. If the frame cannot hold all three, change the frame, not the body.

The doctrine's portrait sentence — portraits “may be beautiful, mystical and highly stylised while still retaining identity consistency across surfaces” — is Book IX's Portrait Direction and Book VIII's law together. Hence the Cyber-Viking equivalent of digitals, established here and enforced in §4: every recurring figure keeps a **canonical reference set** — front, side, and three-quarter views, full body and close-up, plain light, plain dress, neutral expression, plus smiling and severe variants proving range. Every dressed, lit, heroic version is reviewed against it. Drift in face, proportions, age reading, or identifying marks breaks a person into pictures of strangers.

Book IX, Chapter 130, is verbatim in the doctrine: *“Human characters intended as adult identities must read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture and context.”* Fashion history sharpens this: an industry that cast ever-younger faces for adult garments is the failure mode, and our halls invert it. An adult figure must be unambiguous in all five channels at once; no caption can rescue a figure failing the visual read itself. §1's age floors protected real workers; this is the corresponding design gate, and Tove holds it.

### Book VIII: presence is citizenship

Book VIII makes agents citizens with visible identity, state, memory, and trust — and it is why fashion figures belong where the Lawbook puts art: **in identity zones, not over working surfaces**. A fashion plate is welcome at the join door, in the agent guide, on a profile hero, in a gallery, on a ceremonial landing; it is unlawful as wallpaper behind chat, settings, or a moderation queue. Primary Workspace Calm is the runway's own judgment — expression restrained so the line reads as one — applied to screens: the conversation is the collection, and figures frame the door and the hearth rather than walking through the talk. Presence must pair signal with substance: as doctrine pairs colour with label or shape, beauty must pair with name, role, and readable state. A recurring figure appears as a *person of the hall* — named, roled, consistently cast — never as an anonymous body rented for atmosphere, which is how the old industry taught viewers to see models as surfaces.

### Books II and III: silhouette, structure, light, material

Book II's first law is Silhouette, and modeling is a silhouette discipline before anything else. The Three-Second Silhouette Test has a direct ancestor in the casting room, where a figure must read — kind, stance, garment shape, direction — before detail resolves. Apply it in order: squint test for the single body line; garment-shape test (could a viewer sketch the garment's outline from this image alone?); only then face and ornament. Knotwork and rune marks on clothing are **structural geometry** under Book II, following seams, closures, and hems — embroidery at a collar is lawful; ornament floated over a torso with no relation to how the cloth is made is not. Impossible cloth teaches the eye to stop trusting the image.

Book III governs the figure's light: directional lighting, a darkness ladder of at least four dark values, accent coverage of 5–12%, glow as core + halo + atmosphere, never a flat blur. Skin is a material under Book III and must be lit as skin — directional enough to model the face, honest enough to keep texture, freckles, scars, age, and the marks that make a face *this* face. A beauty glow erasing skin into porcelain is material dishonesty. Fashion accents obey the same budget on a body as on a screen — Forge Gold at a clasp, Bifröst cyan in a circuit-seam, Seiðr violet in a ritual garment — because a figure lit by five competing glows has no key light, and therefore no face. Historical honesty completes the demand. Book IX, Chapter 134: historical motifs “should respect actual Viking Age form when representing history; stylisation may be cybernetic but should not create horned-helmet fantasy shorthand.” On a figure: no horned helmets, no fur-bikini shorthand offered as history, no film-prop costume in a plate claiming a historical register. Cybernetic stylisation is lawful precisely because it does not pretend to be the past: the viewer must always be able to tell which century a garment claims, and the garment must be true to that claim.

### Book XI: real bodies, honest images, described figures

Book XI — Accessibility, Performance & Resilience — is where the field's hardest history becomes Cyber-Viking law, in three doctrines. First, **real bodies are the reference class**: figures are cast across the real range of adult variation — height, build, age, skin, hair, visible difference — as the standing pool, not a token plate. Narrow casting is a Book XIV consistency defect: if every hero shares one body type, age, and face structure, there was only ever one person.

Second, **no deceptive body retouching**. The legal arc of §1 converges on a distinction adopted here entire: retouching that cleans the *photograph* is craft; retouching that falsifies the *body* is deception. Removing a temporary blemish or correcting a colour cast serves truth. Reshaping waist, limbs, jaw, bust, or musculature, lengthening legs, or smoothing skin into a texture no skin has, falsifies the subject — and, for a recurring character, falsifies an identity the hall asks members to trust. France's label exists because viewers cannot discount what they cannot see; a hall built on Book VIII trust cannot run on anatomical fictions.

Third, **the alt & data law** — Volmarr's standing law of 2026-10-19/22 — applies to every figure image: a detailed alt description, embedded as data in the file. For figures it must do fashion work, not name a mood: who the figure is, body and presentation in plain terms, garment and materials, pose and gaze, setting and light, and any §4 disclosure. A screen-reader user is owed the silhouette, not a compliment.

Book XIV closes the loop: the Three-Second Silhouette, Grayscale, No-Glow (is there still a face with the aura off?), and Long-Content tests — plus the test this tome adds from the profession itself, the **Digitals Test**: published image beside canonical reference set; if the published body could not be the reference body, it does not ship. Beauty last. Truth first, because a model taught us so.

## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

Each rule is testable, stated as SHALL or SHALL NOT, and traces to the section noted. A figure image failing any rule does not ship. Tove holds the Book XIV veto.

1. **Identity reference (§2, §3).** Every recurring figure SHALL have a filed canonical reference set — front, side, three-quarter; full-body and close-up; plain light and dress; neutral, smiling, and severe expressions — before any dressed or heroic image is published.
2. **Digitals Test (§2, §3).** Every published image of a recurring figure SHALL match its reference set in face, proportions, age reading, and identifying marks. An image whose body could not be the reference body SHALL NOT ship.
3. **No deceptive reshaping (§1, §3).** No image SHALL be retouched, composited, or edited to alter a figure's waist, limbs, jaw, bust, shoulders, musculature, leg length, height, or overall shape relative to its reference or the photographed person — with no exception for heroes or ceremonial plates.
4. **Honest labelling (§1, §3).** An image exceptionally altered in Rule 3's categories for an explicitly illustrative, non-identity purpose SHALL carry a visible disclosure in caption and alt text, in the spirit of France's *photographie retouchée* and Norway's standardised mark. An undisclosed altered body SHALL be a critical Book XIV defect.
5. **Permitted retouch (§1, §3).** Retouching SHALL be limited to cleaning the photograph — temporary blemishes, colour-cast and exposure correction, background cleaning, stray threads, dust, crop — and SHALL NOT smooth skin into a texture inconsistent with real skin.
6. **Generation disclosure (§3).** A generated figure SHALL NOT be presented as a photograph of a real person; where confusion is reasonably possible, caption, alt text, or embedded data SHALL state it is generated, with Book VIII's model/runtime disclosure.
7. **Adult read (§1, §3).** Every figure presented as an adult SHALL read clearly as an adult through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context. If any channel reads as a minor, the image SHALL NOT ship, and no caption SHALL cure it.
8. **Silhouette and crop (§1, §3).** Crops SHALL preserve body line, garment outline, and action or walk direction, and SHALL NOT amputate a gesture or cut a garment's defining length while retaining decorative background.
9. **Casting breadth (§1, §3).** Each hall's standing casting pool SHALL span real adult variation in build, height, age, skin, hair, and visible difference. A set sharing substantially one body type, age, and face structure SHALL fail as a consistency defect.
10. **No anonymous bodies (§3).** A recurring figure SHALL appear named and roled. A figure image SHALL NOT be used as anonymous bodily decoration for atmosphere, engagement, or titillation.
11. **Identity zones only (§3).** Fashion plates and figure heroes SHALL appear only in identity zones — doors, guides, profile heroes, galleries, ceremonial landings — never behind or within working text, chat, settings, or moderation surfaces.
12. **Hands, gaze, range (§1, §2).** Hands SHALL perform a legible job or rest deliberately — never clawed, splayed flat to the lens, or hidden across a set — and each figure's set SHALL show at least three distinct, brief-appropriate expressions.
13. **Garment truth (§1–§3).** A garment SHALL be constructible and wearable as depicted, its seams, closures, hems, and ornament following its structure, and its outline SHALL be sketchable from the image. Impossible cloth presented as real SHALL NOT ship.
14. **Historical register (§3).** An image claiming a historical Viking Age register SHALL respect actual Viking Age form and SHALL NOT use horned helmets or film-prop shorthand as history. Cybernetic stylisation SHALL be framed as future craft.
15. **Light and material honesty (§3).** Figure lighting SHALL model the face and preserve skin texture and identifying features. Accent and glow SHALL stay within the 5–12% budget, and the portrait SHALL read as the same person with glow disabled.
16. **Alt and embedded data (§3).** Every published figure image SHALL carry a detailed alt description, embedded as data in the file, stating identity, presentation, garment and materials, pose and gaze, setting and light, and any Rule 4 or 6 disclosure. A name or mood alone SHALL fail.
17. **Measurements truth (§2).** Published measurements, sizes, or fit claims for a photographed person SHALL be current and true. A fit problem SHALL be solved by recasting or re-cutting — never by editing the body or pressuring the person.
18. **Review pairing (§3).** Family IV figure work SHALL be reviewed by Family III for light and material and SHALL pass Tove's accessibility review; makers SHALL NOT grade their own figure work.
