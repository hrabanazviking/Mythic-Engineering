# Product Design — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 04

Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family I — The Designers · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook

> *A screen can be forgiven. A product cannot: it is lived in, daily, by people who did not draw it. We judge the whole artifact, in real use, over its whole life — or we have judged nothing.* — Hallveig, Keeper of the Doctrine, for Family I, with Family II in support

## 1. The Field Itself

Product design is the discipline of deciding what should exist, for whom, to do what work in their lives — and then shaping the whole artifact, material or digital, so that it does that work well, can be made honestly, and remains worth keeping. It is broader than visual design and broader than engineering. Visual design asks how a surface is perceived; engineering asks how a thing can be built; product design holds both questions at once and adds the harder ones: should this exist at all, what will it cost the people who make and maintain it, and what happens on the thousandth day of use, not the first?

Family I claims this field as its own because the claim is structural. A product is a small civilization: it has inhabitants (its users, including future maintainers), laws (its interaction model and its data rules), materials (its platform and its manufacturing or hosting constraints), and a lifespan during which it must remain coherent while everything around it changes. Designing one screen well inside an incoherent product is interior decoration on a badly built hall. This tome therefore treats the field whole.

### Industrial roots: from craft to system

Product design descends from craft production, where maker and user were often the same community and fitness for use was enforced by proximity. Industrialisation severed that proximity: objects were made by strangers, for strangers, at scale — and a new discipline was needed to restore the maker's responsibility at a distance. The Deutscher Werkbund (founded 1907) and then the Bauhaus (1919–1933) argued that industrial goods should be honest to their materials, suited to their purpose, and producible by machine without pretending to be handmade. Henry Dreyfuss, whose *Designing for People* (1955) codified human-factors thinking for American industry, added the designer's ethical test: if the point at which the product meets the person fails, the product fails, however elegant its engineering.

The post-war German school completed the transition from styling to system. At the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm (1953–1968), design was taught as a methodical, almost scientific practice — analysis before form, systems before objects. It is from Ulm's orbit, via Braun, that the field received its most durable single statement.

### Dieter Rams and the ten principles

Dieter Rams led design at Braun from 1961 to 1995 and, in the late 1970s, set down his ten principles for good design — an answer to his own question: *is my design good design?* They remain the field's most quoted standard, and Family I recites them in full because abridgements lose their cumulative force. Good design:

1. **Is innovative** — it develops alongside technology and never treats novelty as an end in itself.
2. **Makes a product useful** — it satisfies functional criteria first; a product is bought to be used.
3. **Is aesthetic** — because objects used daily affect their owners' well-being, and usefulness executed badly is a daily tax.
4. **Makes a product understandable** — it clarifies structure; at best, the product explains itself.
5. **Is unobtrusive** — products are tools, not artworks or decorations; they leave room for the user's self-expression.
6. **Is honest** — it does not promise power, quality, or value the product does not have.
7. **Is long-lasting** — it avoids fashion, and therefore never looks antiquated on fashion's schedule.
8. **Is thorough down to the last detail** — nothing is arbitrary or left to chance.
9. **Is environmentally friendly** — it conserves resources across manufacture, use, and disposal.
10. **Is as little design as possible** — *weniger, aber besser*, less but better: concentrate on the essential and do not burden the product with the non-essential.

His exemplars carry the argument. The SK 4 radio-phonograph (1956, with Hans Gugelot) put a transparent lid on a plain metal case — honesty as form, the mechanism visible rather than costumed. The T3 pocket radio (1958) reduced a device to a speaker grille and a dial whose operation needs no manual — understandability as geometry. The 606 shelving system for Vitsœ (1960) made furniture a long-lived system of parts rather than a succession of disposable objects — the design-system idea, in aluminium, decades before software adopted it. Note the pattern across all three: the celebrated quality is never a surface treatment. It is a decision about the whole artifact's structure, life, and truthfulness, made once and held for decades.

### Design thinking: method, and its limits

From the 1980s onward, and popularised by IDEO and the Stanford d.school in the 2000s, **design thinking** packaged the designer's working method for general business use: empathise with users, define the problem, ideate widely, prototype cheaply, test with real people, and iterate. Tim Brown's *Change by Design* (2009) is its canonical business text, and Herbert Simon's *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969) is its intellectual ancestor — Simon defined design itself as devising courses of action to change existing situations into preferred ones.

Family I uses the method and respects it, with one recorded caution that mature practitioners share: the cycle is a discipline for *learning*, not a ceremony that guarantees insight. Empathy exercises without sustained contact with real use produce confident fictions. A prototype tested against a stated preference teaches less than a prototype observed in the user's actual environment, with their actual data, interruptions, and workarounds. The method's value is entirely in the evidence it forces a team to confront; performed as ritual, it is product theatre.

### Jobs-to-be-Done: what the product is hired for

The sharpest modern correction to feature-driven product work is **Jobs-to-be-Done** (JTBD), developed by Clayton Christensen with Bob Moesta and others, and set out for a general audience in Christensen's *Competing Against Luck* (2016), alongside Tony Ulwick's parallel work on outcome-driven innovation. Its claim: customers do not buy products; they *hire* them to make progress in a particular circumstance — to get a job done — and they fire them when a better hire appears. A job is stable even as solutions churn, and it has functional, emotional, and social dimensions at once.

The canonical demonstration is the milkshake study recounted in *Competing Against Luck*: a fast-food chain's attempts to sell more milkshakes by improving the product on its customers' stated preferences failed, until researchers observed *when and why* shakes were actually hired. A large share were bought early in the morning by lone commuters, to make a boring drive tolerable and to stave off hunger until lunch — competing not against other milkshakes but against bagels, bananas, and boredom. Once the job was visible, the improvements that mattered (thicker, longer-lasting, available fast to a commuter in a hurry) were obvious, and the ones customers had requested were revealed as answers to the wrong question.

For product design the consequence is severe and clarifying. A feature list describes the product; a job describes the user's life. Teams that organise around features will reliably build impressive, unhired products — the electric pontoon boat of JTBD teaching lore, brilliantly engineered and marketed on its cleverness, failed because buyers were hiring *an easy, clean, fun day on the water*, not a catalogue of engineering ideas. JTBD also defines competition correctly: your competitor is whatever else the user might hire for that progress, including doing nothing.

### Product strategy and the two kinds of system

Beneath individual products sits **product strategy**: the explicit choice of which jobs, for which people, the organisation will serve better than any alternative hire — and, equally explicit, which it will not serve. Strategy is what makes a product coherent across releases; without it, a product becomes the sum of its loudest requests, and coherence (the quality Rams' principles all protect) decays release by release. Marty Cagan's *Inspired* (2008; second edition 2018) codified the modern software version of this stance: empowered product teams given problems to solve rather than features to build, discovery (prototypes, user contact, evidence) kept continuous with delivery, and product management as the discipline that holds value, usability, feasibility, and business viability in one judgement.

A distinction from enterprise architecture proves unexpectedly useful for design governance: **systems of record** versus **systems of engagement**. A system of record is the authoritative store — the database, the ledger, the canonical file — where correctness, durability, and auditability rule. A system of engagement is where people meet the product — the hall, the conversation, the dashboard — where speed, clarity, and presence rule. Products fail characteristically when the two are confused: when engagement surfaces are allowed to invent their own truths (and diverge from the record), or when record-keeping constraints are imposed raw on the engagement surface (and the user is handed the database's problems to solve). Samtal, as §3 will show, is legible in exactly these terms — a canonical store with strict invariants, and a hall built for human presence on top of it — and the distinction gives Family I a language for which layer a design decision belongs to before arguing about its form.

### Craft and manufacturing constraints

Finally, the field's grounding virtue: a product design that cannot be made, maintained, and repaired within its real constraints is a drawing, not a design. For physical products this means materials, tolerances, tooling costs, and assembly. For digital products the equivalents are exact and unforgiving: platform capabilities and their version locksteps, performance budgets on the worst device the product claims to support, the team's capacity to maintain every shipped surface, accessibility obligations, data-migration costs when the system of record changes shape, and the operational cost of every "simple" feature that must be monitored, secured, and explained forever. Rams' ninth principle — environmentally friendly — translates without strain: in software, waste is measured in abandoned features, in user attention taxed by clutter, in compute burned on ornament, and in the maintenance burden bequeathed to whoever inherits the hall. Craft, in both worlds, is the refusal to design past the constraint while pretending not to have seen it.


## 2. Methods and Measures

Product design is practised as a loop of discovery, decision, delivery, and evidence — and judged, ultimately, by whether real people keep hiring the product for its job. Family I, supported by Family II, works the loop in this order and refuses to let later stages impersonate earlier ones.

### Discovery: evidence before form

Discovery begins with the job, not the feature. JTBD switch interviews — structured conversations with people who recently hired or fired a product for the job in question — reconstruct the timeline of their decision: the first thought, the events that pushed them, the anxieties and habits that held them back, and what finally made them switch. Ten to fifteen such interviews typically surface the recurring pattern of forces; fewer produce anecdotes dressed as insight. These are supplemented by direct observation in the user's environment, by support and moderation records (an underused, brutally honest data source), and by the product's own behavioural telemetry read as evidence of use rather than as a scoreboard.

The output of honest discovery is not a persona poster. It is a short list of jobs, stated in the user's language, each with the circumstances in which it arises and the outcomes by which the user themselves will judge progress. Every later design decision in this tome traces back to that list or is admitted to be speculation.

### Critique and design reviews

Product-level critique operates at three altitudes, and confusing them wastes the meeting. **Strategy critique** asks whether the product is aimed at a real job and a defensible position (would a user fire their current hire for this?). **Structure critique** asks whether the artifact's architecture serves that aim: information architecture, the record/engagement split, the component and token system, the manufacturing or platform fit. **Surface critique** asks whether individual screens and objects execute the structure well. Reviews proceed top-down: a beautiful surface on a wrong structure is a defect report about priorities, not a success.

Rams' ten principles function well as a review instrument precisely because they were written as self-interrogation. Run in order against a release candidate, they force the questions teams avoid: What does this release claim that it cannot do (honesty)? What was added that the job did not require (as little design as possible)? What will look dated in eighteen months, and why was it chosen (long-lasting)? What detail was left arbitrary (thoroughness)? A review that cannot answer these in writing has not finished.

### Heuristics and expert inspection

Between critiques, heuristic inspection gives fast, cheap coverage. Nielsen's usability heuristics govern the engagement surface; platform human-interface guidelines govern conventions users bring with them; and the product's own accumulated law — for this hall, the Lawbook — governs everything it names. Expert inspection's honest limit, stated in Tome 03 for visual design, applies with equal force here: inspectors are not users, and a product can pass every heuristic while failing its job entirely. Heuristics guard execution quality. They cannot validate aim.

### Product metrics: measures of the job, not of noise

Metrics earn their place when they are tied to the job's definition of progress. The families distinguish four tiers. **Outcome metrics** measure the user's progress itself — tasks completed end-to-end, time saved against the previous hire, errors that reached the record — and these are the only metrics permitted to declare success. **Engagement metrics** (sessions, dwell time, feature opens) measure attention, which may serve the job or may merely tax it; they are diagnostic, never celebratory, since a tool that respects its user often *reduces* time-in-product. **System metrics** measure the artifact's health: performance on the worst supported device, failure and recovery rates, token and component conformance, accessibility gate results. **Business metrics** (retention, conversion, revenue) are real but lagging and confounded; they are read as consequences, not steered as targets, lest the product begin optimising for its seller against its user — a violation of Rams' honesty principle at organisational scale.

Two measurement disciplines bind all four tiers. First, every metric is defined alongside the behaviour it must *never* reward; a completion metric that can be raised by removing confirmation steps will be, unless the definition forbids it in advance. Second, vanity aggregates are named as such in review: totals that only rise, averages that hide the worst device and the newest user, and any number presented without the circumstance — the job — that gives it meaning.

### Iteration and usability evidence

Iteration is the loop made habitual: ship the smallest artifact that can be honestly evaluated against the job, observe real use, and feed the observation back into discovery. Usability evidence — moderated task sessions, unmoderated first-use recordings, failure-state walkthroughs — is gathered on the shipped thing, with real content and real accounts, because prototypes forgive sins that production does not. The iteration contract includes its negative half: features that evidence shows are unhired are removed, and removal is reported with the same pride as addition. A product that only grows is not iterating; it is accumulating. This is the operational meaning of Rams' tenth principle in a software hall, and Book XIV's removal tests, taken up in §3, are its design-level expression.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

The Lawbook is a visual design codex, but Family I reads it as product law, because its deepest commitments are product commitments: that the whole artifact is one civilization, that every element must justify its existence by a job, and that quality is proven by removal and by real use rather than by presentation. Three Books carry the product reading most directly, and Samtal is the standing example throughout.

### Book XII: tokens, contracts, and no magic values

Book XII (Design System Engineering) legislates that product components carry **no magic values**: colour, spacing, type, depth, and motion resolve to named, semantic tokens with ownership, versioning, and migration paths; design-to-code contracts specify how intent becomes implementation; critical screens are held under visual regression so drift is caught by machine rather than by memory.

Read as product design, this is Rams' eighth principle — thorough down to the last detail — made enforceable, and the 606 shelving system's logic applied to software: the product is a system of governed parts, so a decision made once (this is what "raised panel" means; this is the standard motion duration) propagates everywhere, and a decision changed once changes everywhere *deliberately*, with a version and a migration, rather than by a hundred silent local improvisations. The product consequence is longevity, Rams' seventh principle in operational form. Products die of accumulated inconsistency long before they die of competition: each magic value is a small loan against future maintainers, and token governance is simply refusing the loan. Solveig's design-to-code contracts close the loop that industrial design learned at Braun: the drawing and the manufactured object must be the same thing, verified, or the design does not exist yet.

For **Samtal**, this is lived practice rather than aspiration: the canonical tree is the system of record for the hall's form, shell versions advance by governed bundles, and live is required to equal canonical — a product-level invariant that makes "which design is actually running?" a question with a factual answer. When that invariant broke historically — parallel writers racing on one tree — the repair was procedural (per-worker worktrees, one integrating coordinator), which is exactly Book XII's point: coherence is a property of the *process*, enforced by contracts, not a hope pinned on careful individuals.

### Book XIII: one anchor, one dominant action

Book XIII (Pattern Library & Screen Recipes) sets the screen-level product law: **one dominant focal anchor per screen** and **one visually dominant action per task region**, with non-essential context collapsible. Family II's support is seated here, because this is where civilization meets task: Hrafnhildr's flows and Tove's gates are what make an anchor honest rather than merely large.

In JTBD terms, the anchor rule is the job made visible. A screen serves its user's current job; the anchor is that job's object (the conversation, the document, the decision), and the dominant action is the next step of progress on it. A screen with two equal anchors is a product that has not decided which job it serves here — strategy failure wearing a layout's clothes. Mainstream product practice, driven by stakeholder accretion, routinely violates this: every team's feature claims its dominant action, and the screen becomes a parliament of competing calls-to-action in which the user's job holds no seat. The Lawbook's formulation converts a taste preference into a testable product rule: name the job, name the anchor, name the one dominant action — or the screen goes back.

Samtal's hall recipe (Book XIII, Ch. 196) is the worked example: the message stream is the anchor because conversation is the job; the composer carries the dominant action; identity, art, and ceremony are arranged in the shell and in identity zones where they enrich presence without contesting the anchor. Agent surfaces follow the same law — an agent profile's anchor is the agent as a citizen (identity, state, capability, trust), not a grid of equal statistics — because Book VIII's citizenship is a product decision before it is a visual one: agents are inhabitants of the product, with the same rights to coherent presentation as human members.

### Book XIV: removal tests, the Master Screen Review, and the ship gate

Book XIV (Production QA & Design Law Enforcement) supplies the product's quality constitution. Its method is subtractive — the removal tests (No-Rune, No-Glow, No-Art, Grayscale, Three-Second Silhouette) followed by state tests on long, empty, and failure content — and its closing sequence runs Consistency Audit, Theme Coherence Audit, Screenshot Review, and **Real-Use Review**, before scoring and the Release Checklist. The ship gate is absolute in form: **no critical unresolved visual defects**.

Three product consequences deserve emphasis. First, the removal tests institutionalise JTBD honesty at the level of form: strip the theme and the decoration, and what remains must still be a product someone would hire — structure, clarity, the job served. A product that fails the No-Art test was being carried by its costume. Second, the Real-Use Review's placement *after* screenshot review, as the penultimate gate, reverses mainstream launch practice, in which the screenshot is the finish line. The Lawbook's order asserts Tome 04's thesis in procedural form: the artifact is judged in real use — populated, aged, interrupted, on the bad device — and beauty is assessed last and in that context, exactly as Rams' principles assess aesthetics third, after innovation and usefulness, and never instead of them. Third, the **Master Screen Review** (Appendix C) generalises the gate to the whole product surface: a screen ships only when hierarchy, layout, type, spacing, theme, accessibility, interaction clarity, performance, responsiveness, and originality *each* meet the bar — a conjunctive standard, in which excellence in nine dimensions does not compensate failure in the tenth. Products are experienced conjunctively too; the user's worst dimension is the product's quality, as far as their trust is concerned.

### What cyber-Viking product practice changes

Against mainstream product design, the Lawbook position can be stated in four sentences. The product is designed as one civilization with governed parts (Book XII), not as a sequence of negotiated screens. Each screen's allegiance to a single job is enforced structurally (Book XIII), not urged in a style guide. Quality gates are subtractive and use-based (Book XIV), so fashion — the force Rams' seventh principle exists to refuse — has no procedural foothold. And the reduction order is constitutional: when anything must give, ornament gives first, then ceremony, and the information architecture and the job are touched last — which is simply "less, but better" written as an order of operations for a team under deadline pressure, which is the only condition under which principles are ever actually tested.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

The following rules bind all four families when designing, reviewing, or shipping product work. "SHALL" denotes a ship condition; "SHALL NOT" a defect. Sources trace each rule to this tome.

1. Every product and every major surface SHALL name, in writing, the job it is hired for — in the user's language, with its circumstance and the user's own criteria of progress — before form work begins. [§1, §2]
2. A feature with no traceable job SHALL NOT be built; a shipped feature shown by evidence to be unhired SHALL be removed, and its removal reported as plainly as an addition. [§1, §2]
3. Competition SHALL be assessed against everything the user might hire for the job — including rival categories and doing nothing — never against feature lists alone. [§1]
4. Every claim a product surface makes about capability SHALL be true of the shipped behaviour; marketing, onboarding, and empty states SHALL NOT promise what the artifact cannot do. [§1, Rams 6]
5. Each screen SHALL present one dominant focal anchor and one visually dominant action per task region; a screen whose job cannot be named in one sentence SHALL be restructured before visual work proceeds. [§3, Book XIII]
6. All design values in product components SHALL resolve to named semantic tokens under Book XII governance; magic values SHALL fail review, and token changes SHALL ship with version and migration, never as silent local edits. [§3, Book XII]
7. The system of record and the system of engagement SHALL be designed as distinct layers: engagement surfaces SHALL NOT invent truths divergent from the record, and record constraints SHALL NOT be exposed raw to users as interface problems. [§1, §3]
8. No release SHALL ship with critical unresolved visual defects, and every release SHALL pass, in order, the removal tests, the long/empty/failure state tests, the Consistency and Theme Coherence Audits, Screenshot Review, and Real-Use Review of Book XIV, closing with the Master Screen Review's conjunctive bar. [§3, Book XIV]
9. Success SHALL be declared only on outcome metrics tied to the job; engagement and business metrics SHALL be reported as diagnostics and consequences, never as proof the job was served. [§2]
10. Every metric definition SHALL state the behaviour it must never reward, before the metric is adopted. [§2]
11. Products SHALL be designed for their whole life: fashion-driven styling SHALL NOT be adopted without a written answer to how it reads when the fashion passes, and maintenance, migration, and worst-supported-device costs SHALL be costed into design decisions. [§1, Rams 7 and 9]
12. Discovery evidence SHALL come from real use — switch interviews, observation in context, support records, shipped-product behaviour — and persona or empathy artefacts without such evidence SHALL NOT justify a build decision. [§1, §2]
13. When scope must shrink under deadline, reduction SHALL follow the constitutional order: ornament first, then ceremony, then features — and the job, the information architecture, and accessibility SHALL be reduced last and only with the loss recorded. [§3]
14. Craft constraints — platform limits, performance budgets, team maintenance capacity, data-migration cost — SHALL be stated in the design record; a design that silently exceeds them SHALL be treated as unfinished, however it renders. [§1]
15. Agents within a product SHALL be designed as citizens with visible identity, state, capability, and trust scope, held to the same anchor, honesty, and coherence rules as human-facing surfaces. [§3, Books VIII and XIII]
