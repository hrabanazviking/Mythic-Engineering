## 1. The Field Itself

Fashion design is the design of garments: clothing conceived as a system of material, cut, construction, and meaning, made to be worn on a moving human body. It is often confused with its loudest neighbour, the fashion system — seasons, shows, marketing, novelty — but the craft underneath that system is older and steadier. Strip away the runway and what remains is a set of hard problems that every clothing culture on earth has had to solve. How does a flat piece of cloth become a three-dimensional covering for a body that bends, reaches, sits, and sweats? How much cloth can be afforded, and where should it be spent? How does a garment close, how does it wear out, how is it repaired? And how does it say — to the wearer and to everyone who sees them — who this person is, what they can afford, what work they do, and what they honour?

Those problems give the field its permanent structure. **Material**: fibre, weave, weight, finish, and dye determine most of what a garment can do before a single cut is made. **Silhouette**: the outer shape a dressed body presents — the field's primary unit of recognition across history. **Cut and construction**: the geometry that converts cloth into form, whether by shaping flat pieces, by draping cloth on the body, or by building form into the textile itself. **Surface and trim**: embroidery, woven bands, braid, buttons, and jewellery, which carry cost, skill, and meaning in concentrated form. And **context**: climate, labour, ritual, law, and custom, which decide which of the possible garments is the right one. A designer who cannot speak about all five is not designing clothing; they are sketching costumes for a photograph.

### A short history of silhouette

Silhouette is how fashion history is most honestly told, because silhouette is what changes when the underlying technology and social order change. The ancient Mediterranean world dressed largely by draping: the Greek chiton and peplos and the Roman toga were rectangles of cloth pinned, belted, and folded on the body, their form arising from cloth and gesture rather than from cut. Tailoring — the cutting of shaped, sewn pieces that follow the body's own geometry — developed over centuries and came into its own in late medieval Europe, when fitted garments for men and women, made possible by better cutting, button closures, and the professional tailor's craft, replaced the straighter, looser forms of earlier centuries. From then on, Western silhouette history is a long argument between structure imposed on the body and form following it: the shaped bodices and farthingales of the sixteenth century, the court dress of the seventeenth and eighteenth, the high-waisted, comparatively freed body of the years around 1800, the engineered hourglass of the nineteenth century's crinoline and then bustle, and the twentieth century's rapid succession — the dropped waist of the 1920s, Christian Dior's "New Look" of 1947 with its nipped waist and full skirt, the minimalist and then deliberately anti-fashion silhouettes that followed.

Two lessons from that long argument matter for our work. First, every silhouette is a technology before it is a taste: the crinoline exists because steel and then sprung hoops existed; the fitted medieval cotehardie exists because cutting knowledge existed. Second, silhouette is read at distance, before detail, before face. A figure is known by its outline across a hall, a street, a battlefield. The Lawbook's Distance Test and Three-Second Silhouette Test are fashion's oldest tests, worn rather than framed.

### Draping and flat pattern: the two roads to form

There are two great methods of arriving at a garment's shape, and professional practice uses both, often in the same garment.

**Draping** — *moulage* in the French atelier tradition — works directly in three dimensions: cloth, usually an inexpensive muslin or toile in the working stage, is pinned and shaped on a dress form or a living body, and the pattern is taken from the result. Draping thinks with the hands and the eye. It discovers what a particular cloth wants to do — how it falls, where it wants a fold, how much fullness a bias cut will pour into a skirt. It is the natural method for fluid, asymmetric, and sculptural garments, and it was brought to the centre of haute couture by designers such as Charles Frederick Worth in the nineteenth century and, in the twentieth, by masters of the bias cut such as Madeleine Vionnet.

**Flat pattern drafting** works in two dimensions: the garment is engineered on paper from body measurements, using a system of drafting geometry, and the cloth is cut to the resulting pieces. Its foundation is the **block** or sloper — a basic, close-fitting pattern for bodice, skirt, trouser, or sleeve, fitted to a standard or individual body, from which individual styles are developed by moving darts, adding fullness, changing seams, and grading between sizes. Flat drafting thinks with the ruler and the number. It is repeatable, teachable, and scalable, which is why ready-to-wear depends on it: a drafted pattern can be graded, nested on cloth for economical cutting, and reproduced identically a thousand times.

Neither road is the honest one and neither is the shortcut. Draping without pattern discipline produces garments that cannot be repeated or sized; drafting without draping produces garments that are correct on paper and dead on the body. The mature field holds them together: drape to discover, draft to record, toile to test, fit to judge.

### Textile knowledge: the designer's first literacy

A fashion designer who does not know cloth is in the position of an architect who does not know timber. Textile knowledge begins with fibre — wool, linen, silk, cotton, and their modern companions — each with its own behaviour in heat, moisture, abrasion, and light. It continues through structure: plain weave (tabby), the twills whose diagonal lines give strength and drape, satin weaves, pile weaves, and knitted structures, each producing a different hand, weight, and durability from the same fibre. It includes weight and finish — how cloth is fulled, dyed, calendared, or left honest — and it includes cost, because the price of cloth has decided the shape of clothing for most of human history: where cloth is dear, garments are cut to waste nothing; where cloth is cheap, fullness itself becomes the display.

Colour belongs to textile knowledge, not to a swatch book alone. Before synthetic dyes, colour was chemistry, geography, and money: madder for reds, woad and later indigo for blues, weld for yellows, walnut for browns, lichen dyes in northern regions, and costly imported dyestuffs and silks for the wealthy. A dyed garment announced its wearer's means as plainly as its cut did, and deep, even, colourfast dyeing was skilled work. Any honest reconstruction — and any honest fantasy — must respect that economy: colour was spent, not sprayed.

### Viking Age clothing: what the evidence actually shows

For Cyber-Viking work, the Viking Age evidence is the anchor, and it must be handled as evidence — fragmentary, precious, and easy to overstate. Textiles survive badly. What we know comes chiefly from burial and settlement finds in which cloth was preserved by contact with metal, by waterlogging, or by chance: above all the rich grave finds from **Birka** in Sweden, published in Agnes Geijer's *Birka III: Die Textilien aus den Gräbern* (1938) and reinterpreted for women's dress by Inga Hägg; the settlement and harbour finds from **Hedeby** (Haithabu) in northern Germany, published by Hägg and others; the **Oseberg** ship burial in Norway (buried 834), with its extraordinary survival of woven bands, embroidery, and tapestry; the **Mammen** grave in Denmark, famous for its embroidered textiles; and settlement finds from Dublin, York, and elsewhere in the Norse world. Reconstructions such as the National Museum of Denmark's *Fashioning the Viking Age* project — which rebuilt garments including the richly embroidered outfit from the Bjerringhøj/Mammen material — show both how much can be recovered and how much remains interpretation, a distinction that project itself states plainly.

The established picture, at the level the evidence supports, is this. The core wardrobe for women, as reconstructed from Birka and related finds, comprised a linen or wool underdress, and over it the **smokkr** — the apron-dress or overdress suspended from shoulder straps fastened by paired oval (tortoise) brooches, often with strings of beads between them, and a shawl or cloak over all. Men's dress comprised a tunic, trousers — Hedeby produced notable trouser fragments, including elaborate pleated material — a cloak fastened on one shoulder so as to leave a sword arm free, and leg wraps (*winingas*) bound with garters. Both sexes wore belts, from which knives, keys, purses, and tools hung: the waist was the working surface of the dressed body, its shelf and tool rack. Head coverings, caps, and hoods appear in the finds; footwear was of leather, of simple turnshoe construction.

Construction was economical and intelligent. Garments were built from rectangles and simple shaped pieces — gores inserted to give a skirt flare without wasting cloth — because cloth woven on a warp-weighted loom came in limited widths and represented enormous labour. Seams were sewn by hand; hems and edges, the points of greatest wear, were protected and finished with care. The loom's width, the sheep's yield, and the weaver's hours are all visible in the cut, if you know how to look. That is material honesty in its original form: the garment confesses how it was made because it cannot afford to pretend otherwise.

Decoration gathered where the evidence says it gathered: at edges and points of display. **Tablet weaving** — bands woven with pierced tablets rotated to pattern the warp — produced strong, narrow, richly patterned bands used as garment trim, as starting and finishing borders, and in the famous brocaded bands from Birka, where silk bands were shot through with silver and gold thread. **Nalbinding**, a single-needle looping technique older than knitting in Scandinavia, made socks, mittens, and caps of dense, hard-wearing structure. Embroidery in wool, and in silk and metal thread for the wealthy, decorated collars, cuffs, and cloaks — Mammen's acanthus-like embroidery is the celebrated case. Silk itself appears at Birka and Oseberg as narrow strips and bands: an imported luxury, spent by the centimetre at visible edges rather than by the metre in whole garments. Furs — beaver is documented among northern goods — lined and trimmed cloaks. The pattern across all of it is the one this tome will keep returning to: **plain fields, precious edges; structure everywhere, splendour at the points of honour.**

What the evidence does not show must be said with equal clarity, because Eldríðr's honesty and §5 of every tome in this campaign require it. We do not possess a complete Viking Age outfit preserved whole and labelled; every full reconstruction is a reasoned assembly of fragments, analogies, and choices, and good reconstructors say which parts are which. Colours survive altered by burial; a fragment's present brown is not proof of its worn colour. And the horned helmet — the Lawbook itself names it, in Book IX, as fantasy shorthand to be refused — has no basis as Viking Age battle dress. The historical soul of this field is strong enough without inventing it a costume.


## 2. Methods and Measures

Fashion design is practised as a sequence of material decisions, each of which can be checked before the next is allowed to spend money, cloth, or reputation. The sequence below is the working method of the modern field; the Viking Age maker followed its logic in workshop form, without its paperwork.

**Research and concept.** A collection or a single garment begins with the brief: wearer, occasion, climate, budget, and the story the garment is asked to carry. Designers build reference — historical research, textile swatches, silhouette studies — and the quality of that research is judged by its specificity. "Viking-inspired" is not research. "A Birka-type smokkr, wool tabby overdress over a linen underdress, tablet-woven trim at the neckline, colours from madder and woad" is research, because every clause in it can be checked against a find, a fibre, or a dye.

**Pattern blocks and development.** From the block — the fitted foundation pattern — the designer develops the style: dart manipulation moves shaping where the design wants it; slash-and-spread adds flare and volume; seams are walked to confirm that pieces which must join are the same length; notches and grain lines are marked so that another maker, in another room, cuts the same garment. Grain is not a nicety. Cloth cut off-grain twists on the body, forever, and no pressing will cure it. A pattern is therefore a contract in the same sense Solveig uses the word for components: it promises that form, fit, and repeatability have all been thought at once.

**The toile and the fitting.** The design is first made up in cheap cloth — the toile or muslin — and fitted on the body or form. Fitting is the field's empirical court. Ease (the difference between body measure and garment measure) is judged by kind: wearing ease, the minimum that lets a body move and breathe, and design ease, the extra volume a silhouette deliberately adds. A fitting checks balance (does the garment hang level, or drag toward a fault?), drag lines (wrinkles that point, with embarrassing precision, at the place where cloth is lacking), and movement: the wearer sits, reaches, climbs, and, for our purposes, draws a sword and rows. What fails in the toile is corrected on the pattern, not forgiven in the final cloth.

**Textile testing.** Before cloth is trusted, it is tested — in industry by standardised methods for tensile and tear strength, abrasion resistance, pilling, shrinkage, seam slippage, and colourfastness to light, washing, and rubbing; in the workshop by hand, eye, burn test for fibre identification, and the older knowledge of how a given weave behaves after a winter. This tome cites no standard numbers, because thresholds belong to the garment's own specification; the method is the point. A claim about a textile that has not been tested — its weight guessed, its drape assumed, its dye unexamined — is marketing, not design. Historical work adds its own test: fibre and weave analysis of finds, thread counts, spin direction (the S and Z of spun yarn recorded in the Birka and Hedeby publications), and dye analysis, by which madder, woad, and other dyestuffs have been identified in surviving fragments.

**Historical accuracy standards for reconstruction.** Reconstruction is where fashion design meets archaeology, and the field has settled — in museum practice and in the better living-history communities — on a hierarchy of honesty. At the top: **documented**, directly supported by a named find or depiction, cited to its publication. Next: **plausible**, consistent with the evidence and with documented practice in neighbouring times and places, but not itself found. Below that: **conjectural**, a reasoned filling of a gap, labelled as such. And off the scale entirely: **fantasy**, elements contradicted by or foreign to the evidence — horned helmets, fur bikinis offered as history, machine-stitched "Viking" tunics in polyester — permissible in fiction, fraudulent in a museum label. The *Fashioning the Viking Age* reconstructions model the standard well: they publish their basis, fragment by fragment, and state where interpretation begins. Any Cyber-Viking garment, portrait, or avatar that claims history SHALL be gradable on this hierarchy, element by element, and the grade SHALL travel with the image as part of its alt text and record.

**Judgement in the modern field.** Contemporary garments are additionally judged by measures the workshop era handled by reputation: size consistency against a stated size chart, seam and stitch quality under inspection, performance in wear trials, and — increasingly, and rightly — cost per wear, repairability, and fibre provenance. A garment that cannot be mended is a garment designed to be thrown away, and both the Viking Age evidence and Eldríðr's material doctrine condemn it from opposite ends of a thousand years.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through the Lawbook, fashion design turns out to have been practising Cyber-Viking discipline all along — under other names, in other materials. The Universal Law, that every themed element must have a measurable job and that ornament is reduced before information architecture is touched, is the tailor's law exactly. Trim that does not finish an edge, carry a closure, signal rank, or protect a point of wear has no job, and the history of good clothing is the history of such trim being refused. The garment that survives the removal tests — take away its brooches, its bands, its embroidery — is the garment whose cut was right. The rest was jewellery worn by a mistake.

**Book III — materials, translated to textile and trim.** Book III gives the civilisation its material stack: blackened iron as base, smoked glass as overlay, etched silver as accent, held over a ladder of at least four dark values, with saturated colour budgeted to 5–12% of visible area and glow built in three parts. Fashion translates that stack without strain, because clothing has always been a material stack worn on the body. The **base** is the honest ground cloth: undyed and dark-dyed wools, blackened and smoke-dark linens, leather darkened by use — the iron of the wardrobe, matte, load-bearing, expected to work. The **overlay** is the translucent and the lustrous layer: fine linen veiling, silk strips, glass beads strung between brooches — smoked glass's kin, catching light without generating it. The **accent** is etched silver's true original: the brocaded tablet-woven band, the silver thread worked by the centimetre, the single good brooch — bright, worked, precious, and small in area precisely because it is precious. Forge Gold, Bifröst Cyan, Seiðr Violet, Aurora Green, and Ember enter the wardrobe as dyes and threads, and the 5–12% budget disciplines them exactly as it disciplines a screen: a garment may blaze at the neckline band, the cuff, the cloak's edge — and the great field of the garment stays dark, calm, and wearable. A figure dressed head to foot in saturated colour fails Ljósbera's budget on sight, and reads — as it read in 950 — as someone who did not know what colour costs.

**Ornament discipline: the 1–5% law in cloth.** The Lawbook holds ambient ornament to 1–5% opacity on surfaces, and art density to identity zones. In garment terms this is the documented Viking distribution restated as law: ornament lives at **edges, closures, and thresholds of the body** — neckline, cuff, hem, the strap and brooch points of the smokkr, the cloak's border — and the fields between those points stay plain. This is not austerity. It is how the eye is led: to the face first, along the worked edge, to the hands. It is also how labour is honoured: a tablet-woven band represents hours, and hours are placed where they will be seen. When Family IV dresses a figure — in paint, in pixels, or in a Samtal portrait — the question is never "is it decorated enough?" It is "is the decoration where the body keeps its honours, and is the rest allowed to be cloth?"

**Book IX — adult character design and portrait systems.** Book IX rules imagery, and its bespoke sentence binds this tome directly: human characters intended as adult identities must read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context. Fashion is one of the strongest carriers of that reading. Adult dress signals through fit that acknowledges a formed body, through garments with weight, history, and working closures, through styling that is chosen rather than defaulted. Heiðr's portrait work and the garment work of this tome are therefore one task seen from two sides: the portrait directs the face and the silhouette; the garment supplies the silhouette's lower three-quarters and most of its evidence of a life. Book IX also carries the Lawbook's historical warning — historical motifs should respect actual Viking Age form when representing history, and stylisation must not create horned-helmet fantasy shorthand. Applied to dress, that ruling is Section 2's accuracy hierarchy given teeth: cybernetic stylisation is welcome — a woven band may carry a circuit-like geometry, a cloak clasp may be a machined artifact — but the garment's underlying construction must remain plausible as clothing, and nothing contradicted by the evidence may be presented as the evidence.

**What Cyber-Viking practice changes versus the mainstream.** Mainstream fantasy and much mainstream fashion imagery dress figures by accumulation: more straps, more buckles, more exposed machinery of closure, fur and leather in places where neither survives contact with weather or work, armour and evening dress occupying the same outfit. The result fails the field's oldest tests — it cannot be put on, it cannot be worked in, it cannot be described constructionally by its own designer. Cyber-Viking practice, read through fashion, changes four things. First, **construction before silhouette, silhouette before surface**: the garment must be buildable, then shapely, then splendid, in that order. Second, **material economy as identity**: the civilisation is recognised by how it spends — plain fields, precious edges, dye as wealth — not by how much Norse-flavoured trim it applies. Third, **the body is a citizen, not a mannequin**: per Book VIII, agents and persons in portraits have identity, state, and continuity, and their dress must agree across appearances the way a person's wardrobe agrees — the same cloak clasp, the same band, returning. Fourth, **repairability and wear are designed in**: garments show the honourable marks of use, and nothing is dressed in a material that its own scene would destroy in an hour.

**Samtal applications.** Samtal does not sell clothes, but it dresses people constantly — in agent portraits, profile heroes, avatar frames, the Masters' imagery, and every illustration in which a figure appears. This tome governs all of it. An agent's portrait wardrobe becomes part of her identity record: consistent garments, consistent trim, consistent materials, so that she is recognised across surfaces by silhouette and by her band and brooch as surely as by her face — Book VIII's demand that portraits retain identity consistency, met by fashion's means. Profile and hero imagery follows the edge rule: splendour at the figure's points of honour, calm fields elsewhere, so that text laid near the figure — a name, a capability summary, a trust scope — never fights a costume for the eye. Gallery and media surfaces apply the accuracy hierarchy to captions: a reconstruction-claiming image is labelled documented, plausible, or conjectural, and that label is part of the image's embedded data under the standing alt-and-data law. And when Samtal's people are shown celebrating, working, or grieving, their clothes must pass the fitting test in imagination: could this person sit, reach, row, and embrace in what we have dressed them in? If not, we have drawn a statue and called it a citizen.

Eldríðr, reviewing this section, adds the sentence with which Family III signs the tome: a material that is lied about will be found out — by wear, by weather, by a hand laid on it — and a garment, like a surface, is trusted exactly as long as its materials tell the truth.

## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

Each rule is stated so that a reviewer can test it against a garment design, portrait, avatar, or illustration and record a pass or a failure.

1. Every garment design SHALL be describable constructionally — material, cut method (draped, flat-drafted, or built textile), closures, and order of putting on — by its designer on request. A design that cannot be described SHALL NOT be rendered as finished work.
2. Every design SHALL declare its silhouette first, before surface detail, and SHALL pass the Distance Test: recognisable as its intended figure and station with all trim and detail removed.
3. Materials SHALL be named specifically — fibre, weave or structure, weight class, and finish — for every visible element. Generic labels ("leather", "fur", "metal") without specification SHALL be returned for completion.
4. Materials SHALL be honest: no element SHALL depict a material behaving contrary to its nature (rigid silk, weightless plate, fur in a placement its scene's weather or work would destroy). Eldríðr SHALL hold a veto on material lies.
5. Saturated and glowing colour on a dressed figure SHALL be held within the Lawbook accent budget of 5–12% of the figure's visible area, concentrated at edges, closures, and points of honour.
6. Ornament SHALL be placed at edges, closures, and bodily thresholds — neckline, cuff, hem, strap, clasp, border — and the fields of the garment SHALL remain calm. Overall-pattern ornament across a whole garment SHALL require a stated, documented reason.
7. Trim SHALL have a job: finishing an edge, protecting a wear point, carrying a closure, or signalling rank or identity. Trim with no job SHALL be removed before rendering, per the Universal Law's reduction order.
8. Adult characters SHALL read as adults through garment evidence as well as face and proportion — fit, weight, closures, and styling consistent with Book IX, Ch. 130. Garments that infantilise or default-dress an adult identity SHALL fail review.
9. Any garment presented as historical SHALL be graded element by element as documented, plausible, conjectural, or fantasy, with the grade and its principal source recorded in the work's documentation and, for shipped images, in its alt text and embedded data.
10. No element contradicted by the Viking Age evidence — including the horned helmet and its garment equivalents — SHALL be presented as historical. Such elements in openly fictional work SHALL be labelled fictional in the record.
11. Dye and colour claims for historical work SHALL respect the documented palette and its economy: named dyestuffs or imports, spent visibly and sparingly. Full garments in costly colour SHALL require a stated status justification for the wearer.
12. A recurring character's wardrobe SHALL be continuous: signature garments, bands, clasps, and materials SHALL persist across portraits and surfaces, per Book VIII identity consistency, unless the record narrates a change.
13. Every garment SHALL pass the imagined fitting: the wearer SHALL be able to sit, reach overhead, bend, and perform the work of their scene in it. Costume that only stands still SHALL be redesigned.
14. Garments SHALL show an honourable relationship to wear and repair: pristine, unworn presentation SHALL be reserved for garments the record states are new or ceremonial.
15. Textile claims in documentation SHALL distinguish tested or documented properties from assumed ones. Untested claims SHALL be labelled as assumptions, never stated as fact.
16. Garment imagery carrying text — names, capability summaries, trust scopes — SHALL keep the figure's high-detail zones clear of the text area, following the calm-field rule, and SHALL pass Tove's contrast review.
