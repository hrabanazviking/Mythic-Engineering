# Avatar Design — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 07

Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family IV — The Arts · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook

*I am Heiðr. I think in faces. Before a hall has walls, before a message has words, a face arrives — and everyone in the room decides, in less than a breath, who this is, whether to trust them, whether they belong. This tome is about that arrival: how a represented person — human, agent, or the self made visible to others — is designed so that presence survives a 32-pixel circle in a member list, a crowded hall, a dim screen, and a thousand returns. An avatar is not a decoration pinned to a name. It is identity compressed until only truth can fit.*

## 1. The Field Itself

Avatar design is the design of a visual stand-in for a person (or a person-like agent) in a shared digital place. The word comes from the Sanskrit *avatāra*, a descent — a god taking form so others can meet them. The modern usage keeps that structure intact, even when the theology is forgotten: someone who cannot be bodily present takes a deliberate form so that others can perceive, recognize, address, and remember them. Every avatar therefore answers three questions at once, whether its maker intended them or not: *Who is this? What kind of being is this? How should I be with them?*

The field draws on at least four older crafts. The first is portraiture. For centuries, painted and photographed portraits have negotiated the same tensions an avatar designer faces today: likeness versus idealization, individuality versus type, the instant impression versus the considered knowledge of a person over time. Portrait conventions — the three-quarter view that shows both structure and character, the direct gaze that claims attention, the profile that reads as formal and distant, the careful control of light so the face models rather than flattens — were not arbitrary fashions. They are solutions to legibility. A portrait must be recognized, and it must hold its meaning when it is small, distant, or seen in passing. The second craft is heraldry and insignia: shields, crests, and marks designed to be identified at battlefield distance, in bad light, by silhouette and a few bold divisions of color. The third is character design for animation and games, where a figure must be readable in motion, at many sizes, from many angles, among dozens of others. The fourth, youngest and now dominant, is the interface avatar: the circular crop in a chat list, the bust in a game lobby, the full figure in a virtual world.

The history of the interface avatar is a history of widening expressive range paired with narrowing display space. Early multi-user worlds and forums offered text names, then small hand-drawn icons, then a choice among a fixed gallery. Graphical virtual worlds — *Habitat* in the 1980s, then *Second Life* (2003) and its contemporaries — gave users full three-dimensional bodies they could shape continuously, and discovered both the freedom and the burden of that openness: given total control, many people build not their literal selves but a considered self — younger, bolder, stranger, or simply more legible as the person they understand themselves to be. Games formalized the character creator: a structured sequence of choices — body, face, hair, clothing, markings — that produces a figure who is recognizably *yours* within a world whose art direction remains coherent. Social platforms took the opposite road, standardizing the photograph in a circle or rounded square, which made real faces the default avatar for billions of people and made the *absence* of a face — the grey silhouette placeholder — instantly read as "no one is here yet." Video-game and VR research added the embodied avatar: a body you do not merely look at but look *through*, whose hands are where your hands feel they are. That lineage matters to a 2D hall like Samtal as well, because the expectations users bring — this image *is* that person, for the purposes of this place — were formed across all of it.

Identity and presence research gives the field its working vocabulary. *Presence*, in the virtual-environments literature associated with scholars such as Mel Slater, names the feeling of being in a place and with others, rather than merely looking at pictures of them. *Social presence*, older still in communication research (Short, Williams, and Christie, 1976), names the degree to which a medium lets another person feel psychologically present — warm, addressable, real. An avatar is a social-presence machine: its gaze direction, its distinctiveness, its consistency over time, and its responsiveness (does it change when its person acts, speaks, idles, fails?) all raise or lower the felt presence of the being behind it. Research on self-representation adds a second effect, often discussed under the name *Proteus effect* (Yee and Bailenson, 2007): people tend to behave in line with the identity their avatar projects — a taller, more formal, or more fantastical avatar quietly changes its owner's conduct. Designers do not get to opt out of this. Every avatar system nudges behavior; the only choice is whether it does so knowingly.

No account of the field is honest without the uncanny valley. In 1970 the roboticist Masahiro Mori proposed that affinity for a human-like figure rises with human-likeness — until the figure becomes *almost*, but not quite, human, at which point affinity plunges into eeriness before recovering only at true human likeness. Later work, including MacDorman's translations and experiments and the consistency studies summarized in the sources below, refined the mechanism: mixed realism is a reliable trigger. Photoreal skin beside stylized eyes, a real face with slightly wrong motion, a voice that does not match the face's age — inconsistency, more than realism itself, opens the valley. Motion deepens it: a still figure can pass where the same figure, moving slightly wrong, cannot. The practical lesson the field has absorbed is not "avoid realism" but "choose a level of realism and hold it everywhere" — face, hands, motion, voice, and rendering style in agreement. Deliberate stylization, held consistently, is not a compromise. It is one of the two safe shores.

Customization practice is the field's other great body of knowledge. Mature avatar systems organize choice into taxonomies: the invariant base (species, body architecture, face structure), the expressive layer (hair, markings, clothing, accessories), and the signaling layer (frames, badges, auras, titles) that communicates role, achievement, or state. They have learned, sometimes painfully, several durable lessons. First, defaults are destiny: most users keep most defaults, so the default avatar *is* the system's statement about who belongs here. Second, unconstrained freedom produces illegibility — a thousand sliders can yield a face no one can read or remember — while pure templates produce anonymity; the working middle is strong authored presets with meaningful variation inside them. Third, identity needs persistence: users invest in an avatar because it accumulates recognition; systems that reset, randomize, or silently alter appearances destroy trust out of proportion to the visual change. Fourth, representation is not a feature tier: body types, ages, skin tones, hair textures, disabilities, and cultural dress must be in the base offering, competently drawn, not unlockable novelties. A system that renders some kinds of people badly has made a claim about who the place is for.

Finally, the field inherits portraiture's oldest insight: a face is read as a whole, instantly, and emotionally before it is read in parts. Viewers judge trustworthiness, competence, age, and mood from faces in fractions of a second, and they remember faces by their overall configuration — the spacing and proportions that make this face unlike any other — more than by any single feature. That is why Heiðr's discipline begins where this section ends: get the configuration right, hold it consistent, and let detail serve recognition rather than compete with it.

## 2. Methods and Measures

Avatar design is practiced as a sequence of reductions and stress tests, because an avatar's real life is harsher than its portrait sitting. It will be seen at 24–48 pixels in a scrolling list, half-covered by a status dot, beside twenty other faces, in bright rooms and dark ones, by people who know its owner well and by strangers deciding in one glance whether this is a person, an agent, a moderator, or a threat. The methods below are how working designers earn the right to call an avatar finished.

**Silhouette first.** The character-design method, older than software, is to fill the figure solid black and ask whether it is still itself. A strong avatar — full body, bust, or head — has a silhouette with a clear, ownable gesture: a distinctive hair mass, a hood, a crown of braids, a particular shoulder line. If the black shape could be anyone, the design has no anchor and every later detail is decoration on a stranger. Designers therefore compose the head-and-shoulders silhouette deliberately: hair, headwear, collar, and frame are silhouette instruments, not accessories added afterward.

**Readability at size.** The decisive test is the smallest shipping size, not the hero portrait. The method is literal: export at the smallest size the product actually renders (for chat systems, commonly 32 px and below), place it among real neighbouring avatars at real spacing, and check three things — the face is still a face (eyes and mouth survive as readable marks), the individual is still distinguishable from their two most similar neighbours, and any state signal (online, speaking, away) is perceivable without relying on color alone. Faces survive downscaling when the value structure is bold: clear light-and-shadow modelling of the face's planes, adequate contrast between hair and background, and eyes drawn as confident shapes rather than fine lines that vanish. Fine detail — jewellery, tattoos, texture — is welcome at large sizes but must be designed as loss-tolerant: beautiful when seen, unnecessary when not.

**Expression range.** A static avatar makes one claim forever; a living system asks its portraits to carry state. The method is to author (or select) a small, tested expression set — at minimum neutral/present, active or speaking, idle or dreaming, and distressed or failed — and to check that each reads at small size and that transitions between them do not change the face's identity configuration. Identity lives in proportions and feature placement; expression lives in brows, eyelids, mouth, and head tilt. Systems that let expression edits drift the underlying proportions produce the familiar horror of a colleague's face slowly becoming a stranger's. In motion, timing matters as much as drawing: blinks, gaze shifts, and speaking animation must agree with the portrait's realism level, per the uncanny-valley discipline of §1.

**Customization taxonomy design.** Building a creator or a preset gallery is a taxonomy exercise before it is an art exercise. Practitioners separate choices into the three layers named in §1 (invariant base, expressive layer, signaling layer), then audit each layer: Does the base cover the real range of people who will use this, drawn with equal care? Does the expressive layer offer choices that change silhouette and configuration — the things recognition actually uses — rather than fifty near-identical colour swaps? Does the signaling layer communicate role and state in ways a newcomer can learn in one session? Two measures govern the whole: *time to a self the user accepts* (a creator that takes forty minutes to produce a face its owner tolerates has failed, however powerful it is) and *collision rate* (how often two active members of one hall are visually confusable — the practical ceiling on how large a preset gallery must be, or how much variation a generator must guarantee).

**Consistency and identity persistence checks.** Mature practice treats an avatar as a long-lived identity asset with versions, not a picture. The method: render the same identity across every surface it will occupy — list, profile, card, reaction, notification — and compare. Crops must not change who this is (a tight circular crop that amputates the silhouette feature by which a person is known is a defect, not a crop choice); lighting and background treatments must be systematic; and any regeneration, re-render, or style migration must be tested for identity drift against the previous version side by side, at small size, with viewers who know the person asked to pick them from a lineup. Where identity is generated procedurally, determinism is the measure: the same identity inputs must produce the same avatar, every time, on every machine — a face that re-rolls is a face no one can learn.

**Judgement.** The field judges avatars by recognition speed (can a returning member find this person in a crowded list without reading names?), by correct categorization (do newcomers sort human, agent, and role correctly at a glance?), by felt presence (do people address the avatar's owner as present?), and by dignity (would the person represented, seeing this image at its worst size on its worst day, feel honoured or mocked?). That last measure is not softness; avatars that embarrass their owners get abandoned, and an abandoned avatar system is a failed one by any metric.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through Volmarr's Lawbook, avatar design stops being a gallery of pretty faces and becomes what Book VIII declares it to be: *computational citizenship*. The universal law binds first — every themed element must have a measurable job — and an avatar's jobs are precisely measurable: recognition, categorization, presence, and state. A rune circlet on a portrait that does none of those jobs is exactly the pasted-on ornament the Prime Directive forbids. But a frame that reliably marks a keeper of the hall, an aura whose shape (not merely colour) tells you an agent is working, a background treatment that separates the fire-lit identity zones from the calm message stream — those are structure wearing mythic form, which is the civilization the Lawbook describes: structure ancient, information futuristic, interaction immediate.

Book VIII (AI Agent Identity & Computational Citizenship, Ch. 113–126) is this field's home in the Lawbook, and its chapters read as an avatar specification. Agent Identity Cards (Ch. 113) demand that identity be a designed surface, not an accident of upload. Agent Portrait Systems (Ch. 114) carry the bespoke sentence this family treats as law: agent portraits *may be beautiful, mystical and highly stylized while still retaining identity consistency across surfaces* — beauty is permitted; identity drift is not. Agent Auras (Ch. 115) become, in our practice, the signaling layer rendered honestly: an aura is a state instrument with a learnable shape-language, held to the Book III budget (accent coverage 5–12%, glow built as core + halo + atmosphere, never a flat blur) and to Book VII's rule that presence always pairs colour with label or shape. Model and Runtime Disclosure (Ch. 116) and Human-Agent Distinction (Ch. 122) settle a question mainstream platforms still fumble: in a Cyber-Viking hall, an agent's portrait must never counterfeit a human's. The distinction is not a stigma and not a tiny grey caption; it is part of the portrait system itself — frame geometry, a capability mark in Rúnhildr's glyph language, card disclosure — visible at the sizes where categorization actually happens. Trust and Permission Surfaces (Ch. 121) extend the portrait into the identity card: what this agent may do, stated in a human-readable capability summary, visually subordinate to the face but never hidden. Dreaming and Idle States (Ch. 124) and Agent Failure and Recovery (Ch. 126) complete §2's expression set with doctrine behind it: idle is a designed state, failure is a designed surface, and neither may be faked with a frozen smile.

Book IX (Imagery, Illustration & 3D) governs the portrait as an image. Portrait Direction (Ch. 129) and Avatar Frames (Ch. 131) are Heiðr's chapters: the hero crop must preserve subject silhouette and action direction; art density concentrates in identity zones and stays out of the working centre — in Samtal terms, portraits blaze on the join door, the profile hero, and the People hall, while the message stream keeps its calm dark ground and a small, disciplined avatar column. Adult Character Design (Ch. 130) adds the ruling that any character intended as an adult must read clearly as an adult through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context — a portrait-system requirement, not a stylistic preference, and one that binds generated and commissioned art alike.

Book XII contributes the engineering spine. Deterministic Identity Generation (Ch. 188 in the doctrine's map of the Book) turns §2's persistence check into a systems law: an agent's generated identity is derived, seeded, and reproducible — same identity inputs, same face — so that an agent can be re-rendered at new sizes, new frames, new seasons, and remain themselves. Design tokens govern the portrait furniture (frame strengths capped at three, corner cuts of 6–12 px on the card geometry) so a hundred portraits from different hands still hang in one hall. And Volmarr's alt & data law — every picture carries a detailed alt description, embedded as data inside the file — applies to avatars with special force, because an avatar *is* a claim about a person: the alt text names who this is and what kind of being they are ("Agent portrait: [name], seiðr-worker, violet aura ring marking active task"), so that a member using a screen reader receives the same categorization and presence a sighted member gets from the image. Decorative copies, where they exist, are marked decorative and never intercept input or reading order.

What does Cyber-Viking practice change against the mainstream? Four things. First, it refuses the mainstream's false choice between the corporate photograph-circle and the game character creator: a hall's people — human and agent alike — receive *portraiture*, authored with the same seriousness, inside one frame system that makes their kinds legibly different and their dignity visibly equal. Second, it treats the agent portrait as a citizen's face, not a product mascot: no winking robot heads, no generic glowing orbs standing in for a mind the hall actually converses with. Third, it budgets beauty: Ljósbera's darkness ladder and accent budget mean a hall of fifty vivid portraits still reads calmly, because backgrounds, frames, and auras are drawn from the restrained token palette (void, panel, iron, silver) and saturation is spent on the face and its one signaling accent. Fourth, it submits every portrait to Book XIV's removal tests — grayscale, no-glow, no-art, and the three-second silhouette test — which mainstream avatar pipelines rarely survive: strip the effects and ask whether you still know who this is. In Samtal concretely: the member list, People hall, agent identity cards, join-door hero, and profile surfaces are one portrait system; presence dots grow shapes and labels; agent auras encode state redundantly; and every portrait file in the asset tree carries its alt description in its own metadata, verified, as the standing Samtal practice already enforces.

## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

1. Every avatar SHALL pass the silhouette test: filled solid black, it SHALL remain distinguishable from every other active avatar in the same hall.
2. Every avatar SHALL be tested at the smallest size it will ship at, among real neighbouring avatars; face, identity, and state SHALL all survive that size.
3. No avatar system SHALL signal state by colour alone; every state (present, speaking, idle/dreaming, failed, away) SHALL pair colour with shape, label, or motion that reduced-motion settings can replace without loss of meaning.
4. Agent portraits SHALL be distinguishable from human portraits at list size by frame geometry or capability mark, never by caption text alone.
5. A represented adult SHALL read clearly as an adult through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context, in every crop the system produces.
6. Each identity SHALL hold one realism level across face, hands, motion, and voice; mixed realism SHALL be treated as a defect under the uncanny-valley rule.
7. Identity SHALL be persistent and, where generated, deterministic: the same identity inputs SHALL reproduce the same avatar on every surface and re-render.
8. Expression or state variants SHALL NOT alter the identity configuration (feature placement and proportions) of the underlying portrait.
9. Crops SHALL preserve the silhouette feature by which the identity is recognized; a crop that amputates it SHALL NOT ship.
10. The default avatar and placeholder SHALL be designed with the same care as member portraits; the grey anonymous silhouette SHALL NOT be used as a designed identity.
11. Customization bases SHALL cover the real range of bodies, ages, skin tones, hair textures, and visible disabilities among intended users, drawn at equal quality, in the base offering — never as unlocks or novelties.
12. Aura and frame treatments SHALL obey the Book III budgets: total accent coverage 5–12%, glow constructed as core + halo + atmosphere, at most three visible frame strengths per surface.
13. Portrait art SHALL concentrate in identity zones; working surfaces (message streams, editors, tables) SHALL keep calm grounds with a disciplined avatar measure only.
14. Every avatar image file SHALL carry a detailed alt description naming the person or agent and their kind, embedded as data in the file itself; decorative repeats SHALL be marked decorative and SHALL NOT enter reading order.
15. No portrait SHALL ship until it has passed the Book XIV removal battery — grayscale, no-glow, no-art, three-second recognition — with viewers who were not its makers.
