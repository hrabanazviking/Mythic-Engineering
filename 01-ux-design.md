## 1. The Field Itself

User experience design — UX design — is the discipline concerned with the whole experience a person has when they use a product, system, or service, not only whether its screens look pleasing. The term was popularised by Don Norman during his time at Apple in the early 1990s, deliberately to widen the conversation beyond interface and usability: experience includes first impressions, learning, daily use, errors, support, and memory after use has ended. That widening matters. An interface can be internally consistent and still produce an experience that feels confusing, exhausting, or untrustworthy, because the person did not meet a screen in isolation. They arrived with a goal, a history, a device, a time pressure, and a tolerance that the system either respected or spent.

UX design inherits from human–computer interaction, cognitive psychology, industrial design, human factors, and graphic communication. Its postwar roots lie with human factors: complex wartime systems showed that redesigning the system to fit ordinary human perception, memory, and action beat selecting ever more exceptional operators. Through the 1970s and 1980s, personal computing made that lesson commercial. Xerox PARC, Apple, and Microsoft made graphical interaction ordinary, and researchers such as Ben Shneiderman, Jakob Nielsen, and Don Norman translated laboratory knowledge into principles practitioners could use. By the 2000s, UX was a distinct practice inside product teams.

### Core principles that survive fashion

First, **the user’s goal precedes the system’s structure.** Information architecture organised around internal databases, organisational charts, or engineering modules reliably fails people who think in tasks: *send this, find that, fix this, understand where I am.* Good UX starts from jobs people are trying to complete and contexts in which they complete them.

Second, **people build mental models.** A person forms an internal account of how a system works from its visible structure, language, feedback, and prior experience. Norman’s account of the *gulfs of execution and evaluation* names the two gaps design must bridge: can the person figure out what to do, and can they tell what happened after they did it? When the system’s image — what it shows — supports a wrong model, errors feel like personal failure but are design failures.

Third, **perception is cheaper than memory.** The principle of recognition rather than recall, codified in Nielsen’s heuristics, reflects a robust cognitive fact: working memory is limited and fragile. Visible options, recent items, clear labels, and preserved context reduce load; memorised codes, hidden gestures, and modes that must be remembered increase it. UX that respects the mind puts knowledge in the world, not only in the head.

Fourth, **feedback closes the loop.** Visibility of system status — the first of Nielsen’s ten heuristics — is not decorative responsiveness. A system that saves, sends, loads, fails, or waits must say so in time and in language the user understands. Silence after action is experienced as loss of control.

Fifth, **error is normal and must be designed for.** Prevention is better than messaging, and messaging is better than blame. Norman’s insistence on designing for error, Nielsen’s heuristics on error prevention and on helping users recognise, diagnose, and recover from errors, and Shneiderman’s golden rules on easy reversal and internal locus of control all converge: constrain what can go wrong, make destructive actions distinct, support undo, and write recovery paths in plain language.

### Canonical frameworks

**Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics** (Jakob Nielsen, refined 1994 from work with Rolf Molich in 1990) remain the field’s most taught evaluation lens: visibility of system status; match between system and the real world; user control and freedom; consistency and standards; error prevention; recognition rather than recall; flexibility and efficiency of use; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognise, diagnose, and recover from errors; and help and documentation. They are heuristics — broad rules of thumb for inspection — not a checklist that guarantees usability. Their endurance comes from compression: ten phrases carry a large body of observed failure.

**Norman’s principles of interaction** run alongside them: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mapping, feedback, and a sound conceptual model. The distinction Norman later stressed between affordances — the possible actions an object permits in relation to an actor — and signifiers — the perceivable cues that communicate where and how to act — is especially important on screens, where almost everything is a learned, perceived affordance. A button that looks like text affords reading, not pressing, however its code behaves. *The Design of Everyday Things* (1988, revised and expanded 2013) remains the canonical statement.

**Human-Centred Design as a process standard** is codified in ISO 9241-210, *Ergonomics of human-system interaction — Part 210: Human-centred design for interactive systems.* The standard describes an iterative cycle: understand and specify the context of use; specify user requirements; produce design solutions; evaluate those solutions against the requirements; and repeat until the requirements are met. Its principles insist that design be based on an explicit understanding of users, tasks, and environments; that users be involved throughout; that evaluation drive refinement; that the process be iterative; that the design address the whole user experience; and that the team include multidisciplinary skills. UX maturity, in this view, is not a style. It is whether that cycle actually happens.

**Quantitative laws of interaction** give the field hard edges. Fitts’s law (Paul Fitts, 1954) predicts that the time to acquire a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size — formally, movement time is a function of log₂(D/W + 1), where D is distance and W is target width. It is why small, distant, crowded targets are slow and error-prone, and why screen edges and corners are valuable: they behave as if very wide, because the pointer stops there. Hick’s law (after William Hick and Ray Hyman, early 1950s) describes choice reaction time increasing with the number and complexity of equally likely options — decision time grows roughly logarithmically with the number of choices. It does not mean *fewer options at any cost*; it means grouping, hierarchy, and progressive disclosure have a measurable payoff when a person must choose under time pressure. To these practitioners add Miller’s discussion of working-memory limits — popularly simplified as “7 ± 2,” though modern accounts, notably Nelson Cowan’s work, place core working-memory capacity closer to four chunks — and the practical lesson holds regardless of the exact number: chunk information, do not flood it.

**Information architecture** is the structural half of UX: how content and functions are organised, labelled, and made findable. The canonical text, *Information Architecture for the World Wide Web* by Louis Rosenfeld and Peter Morville (first published 1998, later editions with Jorge Arango), frames IA through organisation systems, labelling systems, navigation systems, and search systems, understood through the ecology of users, content, and context. Card sorting, tree testing, and first-click testing exist because an organisation scheme that is obvious to its maker is frequently invisible to its users. In conversational products, IA becomes hall, channel, thread, and message architecture — the same problem in social form.

**Accessibility science** is not an annex to UX; it is UX under honest conditions. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), maintained by the World Wide Web Consortium, organise success criteria under four principles — Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust — and define conformance levels A, AA, and AAA. WCAG 2.1 and 2.2 added criteria for mobile, low vision, and cognitive accessibility — including target size, reflow, non-text contrast, and consistent help — because field experience showed where earlier guidance missed real barriers. A design that only works for a sighted, mouse-using, high-bandwidth, rested user on a large monitor has not been tested against the field’s own definition of a user.

### Exemplars worth studying

The field teaches by exemplars as much as by rules. The early Macintosh guidelines showed that consistency across applications is itself a feature, because learning transfers. GOV.UK’s design system demonstrated that plain language, tested patterns, and published research can outperform brand-led reinvention at national scale. In each case the admired surface was the residue of research, iteration, and restraint — not a surface to copy.


## 2. Methods and Measures

UX is practised as a cycle of discovery, definition, design, and evaluation, and it is judged — when it is judged honestly — by observed behaviour and measured outcomes, not by the confidence of the presentation that launched it.

### Discovery and definition

**Contextual inquiry and field research** observe people in their real environment. Interviews elicit goals and language; observation reveals workarounds and constraints interviews miss. **Diary studies** extend that view over time. **Surveys and analytics** describe scale — how many, how often, where they leave — but rarely why. Competent practice triangulates both.

**Personas, scenarios, and journey maps** are synthesis tools, not decorations: research-grounded archetypes and narratives that keep a team designing for someone specific. They become dishonest the moment they are invented without evidence and then cited as evidence, and they expire as research ages.

**Requirements in UX terms** are statements about people and tasks that a design can be evaluated against — who must be able to do what, under what conditions, to what standard — as ISO 9241-210 requires. “Fast,” “simple,” and “delightful” are not requirements until they are made observable.

### Design practice

**Information architecture work** — inventories, site maps, navigation models, labelling tests — precedes visual design in mature teams, because moving a mislabelled, misgrouped structure into high fidelity makes it prettier and no clearer. **Wireframes and prototypes** externalise structure at increasing fidelity so that different questions can be asked cheaply: low fidelity for flow and organisation, high fidelity for language, hierarchy, and interaction detail. **Interaction design** specifies states exhaustively: default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, empty, error, success, offline. A screen shown only in its ideal state is a hypothesis about a quarter of the experience.

**Content design** — plain language and task-focused microcopy — is UX work, because most interfaces are read.

### Evaluation methods

**Usability testing** is the field’s core empirical act: representative participants attempt representative tasks while observers record success, time, errors, and reasoning, commonly through a think-aloud protocol. Nielsen’s influential argument that small samples — often around five participants per distinct user group — uncover a large proportion of usability problems in iterative testing is a planning heuristic for formative work, not a licence for tiny summative claims. Moderated testing yields why; unmoderated and remote testing yield scale and speed. Longitudinal and benchmark studies require larger samples and stable tasks.

**Heuristic evaluation** sets several evaluators to inspect an interface independently against Nielsen’s heuristics and aggregate their findings by severity. It is fast and finds real problems, but it finds the problems experts can foresee, not the ones only a particular community, language, or disability will meet in use. **Cognitive walkthrough** tests learnability step by step from a newcomer’s perspective. **Accessibility evaluation** combines automated checks — which catch only a subset of issues, such as missing alternative text or insufficient contrast in code — with manual testing: keyboard-only traversal, screen-reader passes (for example with NVDA, JAWS, or VoiceOver), zoom and reflow at 200% and beyond, and reduced-motion inspection.

**A/B testing and product analytics** measure behaviour at scale in live systems. They answer *which variant performed better on a chosen metric* for *this population and period*; they do not explain why, and they can optimise a local metric while degrading trust or comprehension. Mature teams pair experiments with qualitative follow-up.

### Metrics and thresholds that genuinely exist

ISO 9241-11 defines usability through three outcomes: **effectiveness** (accuracy and completeness with which users achieve goals), **efficiency** (resources expended in relation to that achievement), and **satisfaction** (freedom from discomfort and positive attitudes toward use), always specified for a context of use. Practitioners operationalise them as:

- **Task success / completion rate** — the proportion of attempts that achieve the goal, scored binary or on a graded scale. It is the most fundamental UX metric because it answers whether the thing works at all.
- **Time on task** and **error rate** — efficiency and robustness, read together with success: a fast failure is not efficiency.
- **Single Ease Question (SEQ)** — a 7-point post-task rating of perceived difficulty, useful for locating which specific tasks feel hard.
- **System Usability Scale (SUS)** — John Brooke’s 10-item questionnaire (created 1986, widely published from 1996), scored 0–100. SUS is not a percentage. Decades of benchmarking, summarised by Jeff Sauro and others, place the average score near 68; scores are interpreted comparatively against that body of data, not against a universal pass mark invented per project.
- **SUPR-Q, UMUX-Lite, and related standardised questionnaires** extend measurement toward perceived quality, trust, and likelihood to return.
- **Net Promoter Score** measures stated likelihood to recommend — a loyalty proxy, not a usability measure — and competent teams do not substitute it for task data.

For accessibility, the governing threshold in law, procurement, and the Lawbook alike is **WCAG AA**. Its testable numbers include a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (WCAG 2.x, criterion 1.4.3), non-text contrast of 3:1 for essential UI components and graphics (1.4.11), and reflow without two-dimensional scrolling at 320 CSS pixels width (1.4.10). Target size has tightened across versions as evidence accumulated: WCAG 2.2 criterion 2.5.8 sets a minimum of 24 × 24 CSS pixels at AA with exceptions, while platform guidance and the Lawbook set the more protective 44 × 44 pixel floor discussed in §3. Where standards differ, the practitioner’s duty is to know which one governs and why the stricter one exists.

Performance is a UX metric. The body of work behind Core Web Vitals established that loading and responsiveness shape abandonment and perceived quality; the field therefore budgets Largest Contentful Paint, Interaction to Next Paint, and Cumulative Layout Shift as experience thresholds. Exact budgets are project decisions, but a flow that arrives late or shifts under the finger has failed an experience test. Severity rating binds measurement to action: findings are triaged by frequency, impact, and persistence — cosmetic, minor, major, catastrophic — so a rare annoyance does not outrank a common blocker.


## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through Volmarr’s Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook, mainstream UX does not get rejected. It gets a civilisation to live in — and a stricter landlord. The doctrine’s Prime Directive — *structure is ancient; information is futuristic; interaction is immediate; never a generic app with runes pasted on* — is, in UX language, a demand that identity be carried by information architecture, hierarchy, and interaction behaviour before it is carried by symbols. That is precisely what the field’s evidence recommends and what themed products most often skip.

### The Universal Law is UX law, stated as oath

The Universal Law — every themed element must have a measurable job: help the user recognise where they are, what is active, what can be acted upon, or what changed — restates Nielsen and Norman as a single enforceable sentence. Orientation, state, affordance, feedback: those four jobs are the gulf-bridging work of §1. An element that does none of them is not neutral. Under Hick’s law and working-memory limits it taxes choice and attention; under the Lawbook it is removed. The reduction order — reduce ornament, glow, texture, motion, or symbolic density *before* changing the information architecture — protects the one thing users actually navigate by. Mainstream teams routinely do the reverse, simplifying navigation to make room for brand expression. The Lawbook forbids that trade, and Family II enforces the forbidding.

The coherence conditions — the design must remain coherent in grayscale, at 125–200% text scaling, with long real content, and with atmospheric effects disabled — convert WCAG and responsive practice from a late audit into a design premise. A hierarchy that needs colour or glow to be legible was never a hierarchy; it was paint. This is Tove’s daily work, and it is why she holds the Book XIV veto.

### The Books that govern UX most directly

**Book VII — Communication, Community & Social UX** is the UX book for halls. Its laws — Conversation, Presence, Identity, Context, Continuity, Moderation — and its one bespoke sentence govern Samtal directly: *“The message stream is the hearth. Strong theme belongs mostly in the shell around it.”* In field terms: optimise message width for reading rather than full-bleed display, keep the content centre calm, reveal secondary controls on hover or focus where appropriate, and always pair presence colour with a label or shape. That last rule is WCAG’s use-of-colour principle applied to social presence — a green dot alone tells a colour-blind member, a screen-reader user, and a user in bright light exactly nothing. Presence is information, and information must be redundant.

**Book XI — Accessibility, Performance & Resilience** states the UX floor as law: contrast at WCAG AA or better, touch targets of 44 × 44 px minimum, and reduced motion as a *complete alternate state*, not a dimming. Its chapters on cognitive load and information chunking carry Miller and Cowan into product rules; its insistence that offline, degraded, and error-recovery states are first-class surfaces carries Nielsen’s error heuristics further than most mainstream teams take them. A mainstream product treats its error page as an apology. A Cyber-Viking hall treats failure and recovery as designed rooms the user will certainly visit, furnished accordingly.

**Book X — Motion, Sound, Haptics & Spatial UX** disciplines Ingvild’s craft. Motion must be causal — every animation answers *what changed* — and it is budgeted in three bands: micro motion 80–140 ms, standard transitions 160–220 ms, ceremonial motion 450–800 ms and rare, with structural motion at 260–360 ms in the master defaults. These numbers align with the field’s practical finding that responses under roughly 100 ms feel immediate and that long, frequent animation taxes both patience and vestibular comfort. The named motions — Rune Trace, Bifröst Sweep, Shield Lock, Seiðr Pulse — are permitted precisely because they are named, state-bearing, and scarce. An unnamed ambient animation that signifies nothing fails both Book X and heuristic one, visibility of system status, because it shows movement without status.

**Book XIII — Pattern Library & Screen Recipes** and **Book XIV — Production QA & Design Law Enforcement** together replace the mainstream “design review” with something closer to a trial. Book XIII requires one dominant focal anchor per screen and one visually dominant action per task region — Hick’s law and Fitts’s law expressed as composition. Book XIV’s ship gate — no critical unresolved visual defects — and its subtractive method are the acceptance battery made procedural (see below). Hrafnhildr owns the recipes; Tove owns the gate; neither grades her own work, per the families’ standing protocol.

### The hard numbers, and why they are floors

The Lawbook’s numbers are not styling preferences. Each is a field finding hardened into a token:

- **Touch target 44 × 44 px minimum; standard control height 40–48 px** (Book VI; Appendix A). This exceeds WCAG 2.2 AA’s 24 px minimum because AA is a legal floor, not an ergonomic optimum; it accords with longstanding platform guidance (Apple and Material both converge near this range) and with Fitts’s law: larger, well-spaced targets are faster and kinder, especially for motor impairments, movement, cold hands, and small screens. Compact 32 px controls exist in the defaults for dense, expert, pointer-led surfaces — and they must never become the touch default by drift.
- **Body text 14–18 px; microtext 11–13 px for nonessential content only** (Book IV). Legibility research and low-vision practice converge here. Any essential label, error, or instruction set in microtext has failed before contrast is even measured.
- **Motion bands** as above, with reduced-motion alternatives that preserve meaning (Book X, Book XI).
- **WCAG AA contrast** — 4.5:1 normal text, 3:1 large text and essential non-text — as a minimum, tested in both the bright and dark contexts the theme provides, since a focus indicator that survives a dark panel and vanishes on a glowing one has not survived.
- **Spacing on a 4 px base; panel padding 24–32 px; section separation 48–96 px** (Appendix A; Book V) — rhythm that gives chunking and grouping a physical form, so proximity and separation do grouping work that colour is then forbidden to do alone.

### The acceptance battery as a usability test

The universal acceptance battery reads, to a UX ear, like a ten-item summative protocol compressed into review form: primary task and primary action obvious within roughly three seconds (the Three-Second Silhouette Test — a first-impression and first-click test in all but name); theme recognisable with runes removed; hierarchy usable with glow and background art disabled; all interactive states with keyboard and pointer equivalents; long labels and realistic content not breaking layout; colour never the only indicator of status; working in compact and expanded viewports; decorative assets never intercepting input or screen-reader order; animation reducible without losing meaning; and no detail existing solely because an empty corner felt uncomfortable. Book XIV adds the removal tests — No-Rune, No-Glow, No-Art, Grayscale, Distance — and state tests on long, empty, and failure content. Mainstream UX knows every one of these techniques. What Cyber-Viking practice changes is their status: they are not a researcher’s recommendation that a deadline can waive. They are the ship gate, and Tove’s veto is structural, not personal.

### What Cyber-Viking practice changes — and Samtal applications

Three changes stand out. First, **identity moves from surface to structure.** A mainstream themed product differentiates by skinning standard components. A Cyber-Viking hall differentiates by its architecture — left navigation rail, right context rail, top mast, bottom command bar (Book V’s one furniture vocabulary), its hall-and-channel model, its agent-presence model — so that the No-Rune test passes because there was never a rune doing structural work. Second, **ornament becomes a budgeted, user-controllable material**: ambient ornament at 1–5% opacity, accent coverage held to 5–12% of visible area, and ornament density exposed as a user-facing setting (Book XIII). Mainstream UX debates ornament in the abstract; the Lawbook meters it. Third, **agents are users and citizens of the UX**, not features inside it. Book VIII’s requirements — agent state visible without opening diagnostics, trust expressed as a human-readable capability summary, runtime metadata present but subordinate, human and agent distinguishable — extend UX’s honesty principles to a population mainstream frameworks were not written for. Samtal lives this: the message stream stays calm and reading-width while identity, art, and ceremony concentrate in the shell and identity zones; presence pairs colour with label or shape; living pictures name themselves for screen readers, honouring the standing alt-and-data law; and every dressing of the hall is judged removal-tests first, states second, beauty last. That ordering is the whole doctrine in one queue.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

These rules bind all four families; each is testable and traces to §§1–3. Tove enforces; Volmarr’s word is the only appeal.

1. Every screen SHALL present its primary task and primary action so that a first-time viewer identifies both within roughly three seconds. (§3, Book XIV; §1, visibility of system status.)
2. Every themed element SHALL perform at least one measurable job — orientation, state, affordance, or change — and any element performing none SHALL be removed. (§3, Universal Law.)
3. When theme competes with task, families SHALL reduce ornament, glow, texture, motion, or symbolic density before altering the information architecture, and SHALL NOT simplify navigation to make room for brand or theme expression. (§3, reduction order.)
4. Every design SHALL remain coherent and usable in grayscale, at 125–200% text scaling, with long real content, and with atmospheric effects disabled; failure in any condition SHALL block shipping. (§3, coherence conditions; Book XI.)
5. Colour SHALL NOT be the only indicator of status, presence, error, or selection on any surface; every colour signal SHALL be paired with a label, shape, icon, position, or text equivalent. (§1, accessibility science; §3, Book VII.)
6. Touch targets SHALL be at least 44 × 44 px and standard controls 40–48 px high; compact 32 px controls SHALL be confined to dense pointer-led expert surfaces and SHALL NOT become touch defaults. (§3, Book VI, Appendix A; §2, Fitts’s law.)
7. Body text SHALL be set at 14–18 px; text at 11–13 px SHALL carry nonessential content only and SHALL NOT be used for labels, errors, instructions, or any content required to complete a task. (§3, Book IV.)
8. All text and essential non-text elements SHALL meet WCAG AA contrast — 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and essential UI components — in every theme state, bright and dark. (§2, WCAG; §3, Book XI.)
9. Every interactive element SHALL expose all of its states — default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, error, success — and every state SHALL have keyboard and pointer equivalents with a visible focus indicator. (§2, interaction design; §3, acceptance battery.)
10. Motion SHALL be causal, naming what changed, and SHALL conform to the bands: micro 80–140 ms, standard 160–220 ms, structural 260–360 ms, ceremonial 450–800 ms. Ceremonial motion SHALL be reserved for rare, memorable states and SHALL NOT mark routine actions. (§3, Book X.)
11. A complete reduced-motion alternate state SHALL be provided for every animated surface; reducing motion SHALL NOT remove meaning, state, or feedback. (§3, Books X–XI.)
12. No content centre or message stream SHALL carry intricate ornament behind working text; theme intensity SHALL concentrate in the shell and identity zones, and message width SHALL be optimised for reading. (§3, Book VII; Primary Workspace Calm, Book V.)
13. Each screen SHALL have one dominant focal anchor and each task region one visually dominant action; families SHALL NOT make every element equally luminous or equally weighted. (§3, Book XIII; §1, Hick’s law.)
14. Navigation and hall structure SHALL follow the one furniture vocabulary — left navigation rail, right context rail, top mast, bottom command bar — and SHALL use systematic geometry, including the 6–12 px corner cut and no more than three visible frame strengths. (§3, Books II and V.)
15. Ambient ornament SHALL NOT exceed 1–5% opacity and total saturated or glowing accent coverage SHALL remain within 5–12% of visible area on any screen. (§3, Books II–III.)
16. Empty, loading, error, offline, and failure-recovery states SHALL be designed, written in plain language, and tested as first-class surfaces; no surface SHALL ship shown only in its ideal state. (§2, evaluation; §3, Book XI.)
17. Destructive and irreversible actions SHALL be visually and verbally distinct, SHALL require deliberate confirmation proportionate to their cost, and recoverable actions SHALL support undo or an equivalent reversal. (§1, error principles; §2, severity.)
18. Agent state SHALL be visible without opening diagnostics; agent trust SHALL be expressed as a human-readable capability summary; human and agent participants SHALL be distinguishable at a glance and by assistive technology. (§3, Book VIII.)
19. Decorative assets SHALL NOT intercept pointer input, SHALL NOT enter screen-reader order, and every informative image SHALL carry a genuine alt description — with that description embedded as data in the file, per Volmarr’s standing law. (§3, acceptance battery; alt-and-data law.)
20. No screen SHALL ship until it has passed the full Book XIV gauntlet — the removal tests (No-Rune, No-Glow, No-Art, Grayscale, Three-Second Silhouette), the state tests (long, empty, failure content), and the Master Screen Review — and Tove’s accessibility veto, once exercised, SHALL stand until the defect is repaired and re-tested. (§3, Book XIV.)

## 5. Sources and Honesty Note

Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979), for the original concept of affordances; Louis Rosenfeld, Peter Morville, and Jorge Arango, *Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond* (4th edition 2015; earlier editions as *Information Architecture for the World Wide Web* from 1998); John Brooke, “SUS: A ‘Quick and Dirty’ Usability Scale” (1996); W3C, *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1* (2018) and *2.2* (2023). Background grounding for this tome was checked by web search during the campaign, which confirmed the standard accounts of Nielsen’s heuristics, Gibson’s and Norman’s affordances, and Fitts’s law summarised above.

**Honesty limits.** No statistics, studies, or citations have been invented. Quantitative claims are confined to well-established formulations (Fitts’s and Hick’s laws), published thresholds (WCAG AA, ISO), and widely reported benchmarks (the SUS average near 68), stated approximately where the literature does. If any figure here is load-bearing for a ship decision, re-verify it against the current published standard before the gate.
