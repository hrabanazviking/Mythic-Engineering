# Visual Design — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 03

Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family I — The Designers · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook

> *We do not decorate screens. We build the visible order of a civilization, and then we prove that order still stands when the decoration is taken away.* — Hallveig, Keeper of the Doctrine, for Family I

## 1. The Field Itself

Visual design is the discipline of arranging marks, forms, colours, type, images, and space so that a viewer can perceive structure, understand meaning, and act without confusion. It is older than the screen by millennia — it lives in manuscript illumination, in carved stone, in printed diagrams, in posters and road signs — but its modern form was forged in the twentieth century, when psychology, printing technology, industrial production, and computing converged and forced practitioners to explain *why* one arrangement works and another fails. Family I holds this history as infrastructure. A civilization does not invent its joinery anew for each hall; it inherits tested joints, names them, and teaches them. Visual design, read properly, is exactly that: a body of inherited, testable knowledge about how human perception organises the visible world.

### Perception first: the Gestalt foundation

The field's scientific root is Gestalt psychology, developed in Germany in the 1910s and 1920s by Max Wertheimer, Kurt Koffka, and Wolfgang Köhler. Its central claim — that the whole is perceived before, and differently from, the sum of its parts — sounds like a slogan until it is made operational, which the Gestalt researchers did through a set of grouping principles that remain in daily use a century later.

**Proximity** states that elements placed near one another are perceived as a group. **Similarity** states that elements sharing colour, shape, size, or orientation are perceived as related. **Continuity** (or good continuation) states that the eye follows lines and curves past their intersections, preferring smooth paths. **Closure** states that the mind completes incomplete figures, perceiving a whole shape from partial contours. **Figure–ground** describes the fundamental separation of an object of attention from its background, without which nothing can be seen at all. Later additions — **common region** (elements inside a shared boundary belong together), **common fate** (elements moving together belong together), and **connectedness** (elements joined by a line or bar are grouped most strongly of all) — completed a practical toolkit.

These are not stylistic preferences. They are descriptions of pre-attentive processing: the grouping happens before conscious reading begins, in the first fractions of a second of looking. That is why they matter to Family I above almost any other inheritance. If proximity in a layout says two controls belong together while the underlying system says they do not, the layout is lying, and the lie is told faster than the user can reason against it. Every composition decision is therefore either an act of truth-telling or an act of deception at the perceptual level, whether the designer intended it or not.

### Hierarchy, composition, and the ordering of attention

If Gestalt principles explain grouping, **visual hierarchy** explains sequence: what is seen first, second, and last. Hierarchy is built from a small set of levers — size, weight, colour and contrast, position, spacing, and isolation — applied in deliberate ratios. A composition without hierarchy is not democratic; it is illegible, because the viewer's limited attention is spent on searching rather than understanding.

Composition theory, drawn from painting, photography, and graphic design, supplies the structural patterns. The **grid** — systematised for print by Josef Müller-Brockmann in *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981) — divides a surface into columns and fields so that disparate content can share one spatial logic. Balance may be symmetrical or asymmetrical; what matters is that visual weight is accounted for. Focal points are established, then subordinated elements are arranged in flow lines that carry the eye through the intended sequence. **Negative space** — the unmarked area around and between subjects — is treated by mature practice not as emptiness but as a material: it separates, calms, and gives the marked elements their legibility. Jan Tschichold's work on asymmetric typography in *The New Typography* (1928) and the Swiss International Style that followed demonstrated that restraint, alignment, and whitespace could carry an entire visual identity without ornament — a demonstration Family I returns to often, because it proves identity can live in structure alone.

### Semiotics: marks that mean

Visual design is also a language, and semiotics — the study of signs, founded in the work of Ferdinand de Saussure and Charles Sanders Peirce — is its grammar of meaning. Peirce's triad remains the working distinction: an **icon** signifies by resemblance (a picture of a ship), an **index** signifies by causal connection (a gauge needle, a trail of smoke), and a **symbol** signifies by convention (a letter, a flag, a rune). Confusing these categories produces real design failures. An icon asked to do a symbol's job will be misread by anyone outside the resemblance; a symbol treated as decoration loses the convention that gave it power.

This is the scholarly ground beneath the Lawbook's rune doctrine. A rune is a symbol in Peirce's strict sense: its meaning is conventional, historical, and specific. Used as a generic angular texture, it is not merely inaccurate — it has been demoted from symbol to noise, and a literate viewer reads the demotion instantly. Semiotics also explains why consistency is a moral as well as practical requirement: conventions only function when they are held stable. A mark that means "active" on one screen and "sacred" on another has ceased to be a symbol and become a riddle.

### Information design and Tufte's discipline

Where visual design meets data, it becomes information design, and its modern canon is Edward Tufte's. In *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983), *Envisioning Information* (1990), *Visual Explanations* (1997), and *Beautiful Evidence* (2006), Tufte established principles that Family I treats as settled law for any surface carrying numbers or comparisons.

First, **graphical excellence** is the well-designed presentation of interesting data with clarity, precision, and efficiency. Second, the **data-ink ratio**: ink devoted to data should be maximised and ink devoted to non-data decoration — his famous term is **chartjunk** — should be erased, because every decorative mark competes with the evidence. Third, displays should show **comparisons**, demonstrate **causality**, be **multivariate** rather than flattened, integrate words, numbers, and images instead of segregating them, and document their sources. Fourth, **small multiples** — repeated frames differing in one variable — let the eye perform comparisons that memory cannot. Fifth, in his later formulation, design should pursue the standard he summarised as "simple design, intense content."

Tufte's exemplars are instructive. He repeatedly praised Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 flow map of Napoleon's Russian campaign, which encodes army size, geography, direction, temperature, and time in a single figure — six variables, no chartjunk, devastating clarity. The lesson for a screen designer is not nostalgia for lithography. It is that density is not the enemy of clarity; *unstructured* density is. A surface can carry a great deal when every element has a job and the structure is honest.

### Design systems thinking

The most recent stratum of the field is **design systems thinking**: the shift from designing individual screens to designing the reusable language from which screens are assembled. Its lineage runs through Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* (1977), which argued that good environments are built from named, reusable patterns at multiple scales; through the component architectures of modern software; and into contemporary practice codified in works such as Brad Frost's *Atomic Design* (2016), which layers atoms, molecules, organisms, templates, and pages into one construction logic.

A design system is not a sticker sheet. At its mature form it comprises tokens (named values for colour, spacing, type, motion, depth), components with defined states, composition rules, and governance: who may change what, how versions migrate, and how conformance is verified. The intellectual move is the one Family I is named for. The unit of design stops being the page and becomes the *civilization*: the enduring set of laws, materials, and joints within which many builders, over many years, can produce work that is recognisably one culture and never a collage of individual tastes. Solveig's forge-engineering is this idea with its sleeves rolled up — contracts between design intent and code, so that the system survives the designer who drew it.

Taken together, the field's inheritance is coherent: perception groups (Gestalt), attention sequences (hierarchy and composition), marks mean (semiotics), evidence must not be decorated away (Tufte), and all of it must be systematised to endure (design systems). Visual design is not the art of making screens pretty. It is the engineering of what can be understood at a glance — and of what remains understandable on the thousandth glance.


## 2. Methods and Measures

A field earns the name *discipline* when its judgements can be checked by someone other than the person who made them. Visual design has developed a working set of methods for this, and Family I uses them in a deliberate order: reason from principles, inspect against heuristics, observe real use, and measure where measurement is honest.

### Critique method

The design critique is the field's oldest quality instrument. Done properly, it is not a referendum on taste. A disciplined critique proceeds in stages: the designer states the intent and the constraints; reviewers describe what they actually perceive before judging it ("the eye lands on the banner, not the task"); perception is checked against principle (which Gestalt grouping is operating here, and does it match the system's real structure?); and only then are changes proposed, each tied to a named problem. Critique vocabulary matters. "I don't like it" terminates thought; "proximity groups the destructive action with the safe ones" starts repair.

Two guards keep critique honest. The first is **real content**: compositions are reviewed with long labels, worst-case data, translated strings, and populated lists, never with placeholder serenity, because most layout failures are content failures in disguise. The second is **subtraction as a diagnostic**: temporarily remove an element and observe what information is lost. If nothing is lost, the element was decoration wearing a uniform.

### Design reviews and heuristic inspection

Formal design reviews extend critique into a gate. Reviewers inspect a surface against established heuristic sets — in interface work, most famously Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, and in visual work, principle checklists derived from Gestalt grouping, hierarchy, alignment, and consistency. Heuristic inspection is fast and catches systematic faults (inconsistent spacing scales, competing focal points, colour-only coding) before any user is exposed to them. Its limit is equally well known: experts inspecting a design are not users performing a task, so heuristic cleanliness is a necessary condition, never a sufficient one.

Visual-specific review instruments include the squint test (blur or step back: does the hierarchy survive as pure weight and position?), the grayscale pass (does structure survive without hue?), and the thumbnail pass (does the composition read at small size, as it will in recents screens, search results, and notifications?). Each is a cheap simulation of a real perceptual condition.

### Usability evidence

The field's empirical anchor is observed use. Formative usability testing — a small number of representative participants performing real tasks while thinking aloud — reliably surfaces the failures that inspection misses: the grouping the designer intended but the user never saw, the hierarchy that inverted under real content, the symbol that meant something else entirely to its audience. Summative measures (task completion, time on task, error rate, standardised satisfaction scores) belong to mature products and comparative questions; they are poor instruments for diagnosing *why* a composition fails, which is why qualitative observation remains primary in design practice.

Eye-tracking and attention analytics can supplement observation, but Family I treats them with suspicion proportionate to their seductiveness. A heat map shows where eyes went, not what was understood; it documents attention, not comprehension, and must never be presented as proof of clarity.

### Measures that are honest — and those that are not

Visual design's measurable claims are mostly *structural*, and the field is healthiest when it says so. Honest measures include: contrast ratios against WCAG thresholds; type sizes and target sizes against platform minima; spacing and sizing conformance to the declared scale; token conformance (the proportion of rendered values that come from the system rather than ad-hoc choices); and visual regression results, where automated screenshot comparison catches unintended drift between versions. Behavioural measures — time to first successful action, mis-tap rates on grouped controls, search-versus-browse ratios — connect composition to outcomes and are the strongest evidence a layout is working.

Dishonest measures are equally recognisable: preference votes between two polished mock-ups presented without tasks; aesthetic ratings of empty screens; and any metric gathered on content shorter, cleaner, or more English than the content the product will actually carry. The method section of this tome therefore ends where §3 begins: the Lawbook's acceptance battery is, in effect, a codification of the field's honest methods into a single repeatable gate — and its removal tests are the subtraction diagnostic, formalised and given teeth.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through the Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook, the field described above does not change its facts. Gestalt grouping still groups; symbols still mean by convention; chartjunk still lies. What changes is the *constitution* under which those facts are applied. Mainstream visual design typically treats its principles as guidance, weighed afresh on each project against brand, fashion, and deadline. The Lawbook treats them as law: numbered, budgeted, and gated. Family I's reading follows.

### The Universal Law as visual constitution

The doctrine's Universal Law states that every themed element must have a measurable job — help the user recognise where they are, what is active, what can be acted upon, or what changed — and that when theme competes with task, ornament, glow, texture, motion, and symbolic density are reduced *before* information architecture is touched. In the vocabulary of §1, this is Tufte's data-ink discipline generalised from charts to entire surfaces: every mark must earn its ink, and the reduction order is fixed in advance so that no deadline can quietly reverse it. The coherence conditions — legible in grayscale, at 125–200% text scaling, with long real content, with atmospheric effects disabled — convert the squint, grayscale, and thumbnail tests of §2 from good practice into ship conditions.

### Book II: grammar and geometry, numbered

Book II (Visual Grammar & Geometry) is where the Lawbook most directly legislates composition. Its hard numbers are few and therefore enforceable: a **corner cut of 6–12 px** on standard controls, so the chamfer becomes the civilization's signature edge rather than a per-screen improvisation; a maximum of **three visible frame strengths**, so containment hierarchy — the common-region principle, legislated — can never blur into a dozen competing borders; and **ambient ornament opacity of 1–5%**, which budgets texture the way Tufte budgets ink.

Rannveig's composition doctrine lives here. Knotwork and dragon-weave are structural — they mark boundaries and joins, which is to say they perform the Gestalt work of common region and connectedness — while runes sit as **nodes on circuit lines**, connection points in a system, rather than scattered as lettering. Negative space is named a designed material, which ratifies the Swiss lesson of §1: the calm between elements is built, not left over. Where mainstream practice reaches for a new decorative motif per feature, cyber-Viking practice asks the older question: what structural job does this geometry do, and which of the three frame strengths is it allowed to be?

### Book V: composition at hall scale

Book V (Grid, Layout & Composition) supplies the architecture within which Book II's grammar operates: twelve columns on desktop, sixteen on ultrawide; outer margins of 32–72 px; section gaps of 32–96 px. Three named disciplines carry the Cyber-Viking difference.

The **Three-Border Limit** caps nested containment. Mainstream interfaces routinely nest cards inside panels inside bordered sections until proximity and common region contradict each other and the user can no longer tell what belongs to what. The limit forces a choice: if a fourth border seems necessary, the structure — not the border count — is wrong.

**Primary Workspace Calm** declares the centre of the screen the calm zone, with intensity held at the edges. This is figure–ground discipline at architectural scale: the working content is the figure, and it can only remain the figure if the ground refuses to compete. In Samtal this is already standing taste law, ratified by the Lawbook in Book VII's single bespoke sentence: *"The message stream is the hearth. Strong theme belongs mostly in the shell around it."* The message column stays dark, readable, and unornamented; identity, art, and ceremony live in the shell — rails, headers, profile zones — where they can be rich without taxing reading.

**One furniture vocabulary** — left navigation rail, right context rail, top mast, bottom command bar, the same for user and developer surfaces — is semiotics applied to architecture. Position becomes a stable symbol: users learn once where navigation, context, and commands live, and that convention holds across every hall. Mainstream products routinely break this for novelty, spending the user's learned fluency on a surprise. Cyber-Viking practice refuses the trade: novelty is spent on content and capability, never on relocating the furniture.

### What changes, stated plainly — and Samtal

Against mainstream visual design, then, Cyber-Viking practice changes four things. First, principles become budgets: ornament, accent, frames, and motion carry numbers, not vibes. Second, identity moves from surface to structure: the No-Rune Test — theme recognisable with runes removed — makes the Swiss proof (identity surviving ornament removal) a formal gate. Third, the calm centre is protected by law rather than by a designer's vigilance on a good day. Fourth, review is subtractive before it is additive: Book XIV's removal tests run before beauty is discussed.

For **Samtal**, the applications are concrete. The message stream is audited as figure against a calm ground: any proposed shell art that raises the centre's visual weight fails Primary Workspace Calm regardless of its beauty. Agent identity (Book VIII) is a hierarchy problem: state must be visible without opening diagnostics, which means agent presence, activity, and trust scope must win their place in the composition through size, position, and grouping — not through glow competing with the conversation. Cards, media tiles, and tarot imagery live in identity zones where art density is lawful, and each carries the standing alt-and-data law: a picture's description travels inside the file, because a visual system that only sighted users can perceive has failed figure–ground for everyone else. Finally, every Samtal screen is a Rannveig drawing before it is anyone's painting: axis, ribs, three borders at most, one dominant focal idea per region — then, and only then, light and letter.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

The following rules bind all four families when producing or reviewing visual design. Each is testable; "SHALL" denotes a ship condition, "SHALL NOT" a defect. Sources in brackets trace the rule to this tome.

1. Every themed or decorative element SHALL be assignable, in review, to one measurable job — orientation, active-state signalling, affordance, or change signalling — or SHALL be removed. [§3, Universal Law]
2. When theme and task compete, reviewers SHALL reduce ornament, glow, texture, motion, and symbolic density before permitting any change to information architecture. [§3]
3. Proximity, similarity, and common region in a layout SHALL match the underlying system's real groupings; a layout whose Gestalt grouping contradicts system structure SHALL be treated as a functional defect, not a style choice. [§1]
4. Each region of a screen SHALL have exactly one dominant focal idea; no two elements in a region SHALL carry equal maximum visual weight. [§1, §3]
5. Standard controls SHALL use a corner cut of 6–12 px, and no screen SHALL display more than three visible frame strengths. [§3, Book II]
6. Ambient ornament SHALL NOT exceed 5% opacity, and ornament SHALL NOT appear behind working text in the primary workspace. [§3, Books II and V]
7. Containment SHALL obey the Three-Border Limit; a design requiring a fourth nested border SHALL be restructured rather than granted an exception. [§3, Book V]
8. Navigation, context, and command functions SHALL occupy the single furniture vocabulary — left rail, right context rail, top mast, bottom command bar — on user and developer surfaces alike. [§3, Book V]
9. Runes SHALL be used only as verified semantic marks or as nodes on circuit lines, with form, period, transliteration, and symbolic role checked before use; runes SHALL NOT be used as a substitute alphabet or as generic texture. [§1, §3]
10. Colour SHALL NOT be the sole indicator of any status; every colour-coded state SHALL be paired with a label, shape, position, or text difference. [§2, §3]
11. Data-bearing surfaces SHALL maximise data-ink: non-data decoration on charts and telemetry SHALL be removed before any data element is restyled, and real telemetry only SHALL be displayed — never invented futuristic numbers. [§1, §3]
12. Every screen SHALL pass, in order, the No-Rune Test, the No-Glow Test, the No-Art Test, the Grayscale Test, and the Three-Second Silhouette Test before aesthetic review begins. [§2, §3, Book XIV]
13. Compositions SHALL be reviewed with long labels, realistic populated content, and 125–200% text scaling; a design validated only on short placeholder content SHALL NOT ship. [§2, §3]
14. All rendered visual values — colour, spacing, type size, corner geometry, motion duration — SHALL resolve to named design tokens; magic values in product surfaces SHALL fail review. [§1, §3]
15. Negative space SHALL be specified as part of the composition (margins 32–72 px, section gaps 32–96 px on desktop grids of twelve columns, sixteen ultrawide), not left as unexamined remainder. [§1, §3, Book V]
16. Every picture SHALL carry a detailed alt description, with that description embedded as data in the file itself, and decorative assets SHALL NOT intercept pointer input or screen-reader order. [§3]
