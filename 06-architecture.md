# Architecture — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 06

> Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family I — The Designers · Support: Family IV — The Arts · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook

*I am Rannveig, Composition Architect of Family I. I think in longhouses and ships: axis, ribs, calm centers. What follows is architecture read honestly — how human beings learned to give space a legible order — carried, without cosplay, into the digital halls we now build. If a screen cannot be walked in the mind the way a good hall can be walked with the feet, it has failed before its colours are chosen.*

## 1. The Field Itself

Architecture is the deliberate ordering of space for human life. It is at once a technical discipline — structure, climate, material, circulation — and a cultural one: every building states, in timber or stone or pixels, what its makers believed about gathering, privacy, rank, work, and welcome. The architect's raw materials are not only walls and roofs. They are axis and threshold, light and shadow, sequence and pause, proportion and repetition. A plan is a promise about movement. A section is a promise about air and light. An elevation is a promise about how a place will meet the world.

That double nature — engineering and meaning held in one act — is why architecture matters so much to Cyber-Viking design. The Lawbook's Prime Directive insists that a cyber-Viking interface must look as if an advanced Norse-influenced technological culture developed its own design language from first principles, never as a generic app with runes pasted on top. Architecture is the field that proves such a thing is possible, because Norse builders themselves did it: they carried a shipwright's logic onto land and raised halls and churches that were structurally inventive, spatially clear, and unmistakably theirs — without needing a dragon head on every joint to be recognised.

### The longhouse: one room, rightly ordered

The Viking Age longhouse — *langhús* — is the foundational spatial type of the Norse world, and it repays close study precisely because it is so restrained. Reconstructions and excavations, summarised in works such as Holger Schmidt's *Building Customs in Viking Age Denmark* (1994), describe houses typically 5 to 7 metres wide and anywhere from 15 to 75 metres long, the length rising with the wealth and standing of the household. The proportions matter. A longhouse is not long by accident. Its length is its social diagram: a single elongated volume, entered from the long side or the gable end, organised along a central axis, with function graduating along that axis rather than being chopped into sealed boxes.

Inside, paired rows of interior posts — the roof-bearing staves — divided the space into a broad central nave and narrower side aisles. Benches ran along the walls. The central hearth — the *langeldr*, the long fire — sat on the axis, and everything else took its meaning from distance to that fire: the high seat near it, the working and sleeping places further off, the byre and storage toward the ends in the larger farmhouses. There was, in the early longhouse, no chimney; smoke rose and found its way through openings in the roof, which meant the upper volume was a smoky, dim, shared atmosphere and the clear, breathable, usable zone was low and close to the floor and fire. Light was scarce and therefore precious — small openings, firelight, lamplight — and the architecture spent it deliberately.

Three lessons live in that room, and I return to them constantly. First, **one dominant centre organises everything else**. The hearth is not one feature among many; it is the reason the benches face inward, the reason the axis exists. Second, **structure is visible and rhythmic**. The paired posts do not hide inside walls; they stand in the room at regular intervals, and that rhythm tells an occupant where they are along the length without any sign. Third, **hierarchy is graduated, not walled**. Status, warmth, and privacy change by degree along the axis rather than by locked doors. You read the room by walking it.

Curved walls and a slightly boat-like, double-curved roof profile appear in many reconstructions — the Avaldsnes longhouse in Norway, rebuilt in 1997 from a house excavated at Oma in Rogaland and dated to about 950, is 24 metres long, built with trestle construction, curved walls, and a shingled roof. Scholars argue about how universal that curvature was, and honest work must admit the argument. What is not in dispute is the type itself: elongated, post-built, hearth-centred, and immediately legible to anyone who entered it.

### The ship: architecture that moves

If the longhouse is Norse architecture at rest, the longship is Norse architecture under tension — and the Lawbook is right to pair them, in Book II, as Longhouse Composition and Ship-Built Composition. A Viking ship is a shell built before it is a frame filled: overlapping strakes riveted together — clinker construction — producing a hull that is light, flexible, and strong precisely because its parts work together under stress. Ribs follow the shell; they do not dictate it. The keel is a true axis, a single grown timber from stem to stern, and every other member takes its position from it. The ship has a fierce economy: nothing aboard is without a job, weight is an enemy, and yet the result — the Oseberg and Gokstad ships are the famous surviving witnesses — is beautiful, because the beauty is the visible record of problems solved well.

Ship logic teaches a different set of lessons from hall logic. Where the longhouse teaches centre and graduation, the ship teaches **economy under constraint, structural honesty, and directional clarity**. A ship always knows which way it is going. Its composition has a prow and a stern, a clear forward, and its symmetry is not decorative — it is hydrodynamic. Applied to composition, ship-building argues for layouts that have a direction of travel, that shed unnecessary weight, and whose strength comes from how parts overlap and lock rather than from bulk added on top.

### The stave church: inherited craft, new programme

The stave churches of Norway — Borgund, Urnes, Heddal, and the two dozen others that survive from a population once counted, at its medieval peak, in the many hundreds and by some estimates over a thousand — are the great proof that a building tradition can absorb a wholly new programme without surrendering its structural soul. The name comes from *stafr*, post: load-bearing vertical staves, raised first on stone sills to lift them clear of ground rot — a crucial technical step away from earlier earth-bound post construction, whose posts rotted where they met the soil — with wall planks slotted between them and a complex, tiered roof carried above.

These were Christian churches, with a liturgical plan their pagan ancestors never needed, built by craftsmen whose hands knew shipbuilding and hall-building. The result is neither a stone Romanesque church translated clumsily into wood nor a longhouse with a cross nailed to it. It is a genuinely new type: a tall central nave ringed by an ambulatory, staves rising like a grove, carved portals whose ornament is concentrated at thresholds, and roofs stacked in diminishing tiers that shed weather and announce the building from far off. Ornament, notably, is not spread evenly. It gathers at doors and gable ends — at the points of entry and identity — and leaves the great wall surfaces comparatively plain. That distribution, arrived at by medieval carpenters for their own reasons, is precisely the discipline the Lawbook demands: art density concentrated in identity zones, working surfaces left calm.

### Pattern language: Christopher Alexander

In 1977 Christopher Alexander, with Sara Ishikawa and Murray Silverstein, published *A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction* — 253 patterns, each naming a recurring spatial problem, the forces at work in it, and a solution stated plainly enough to be built. Patterns nest: a large pattern — a town, a neighbourhood, a building's organisation — is held up by smaller ones, down to the depth of a window seat and the placement of a shelf. No pattern stands alone; Alexander is explicit that each pattern exists only insofar as it is supported by larger patterns that contain it, patterns of the same size around it, and smaller patterns inside it.

Whatever one thinks of Alexander's later metaphysics in *The Nature of Order* (2002–2005), the pattern method itself changed design far beyond architecture — software design patterns and much of interaction design descend from it directly — because it solved a real problem: how to transmit spatial wisdom without reducing it to either a style manual or a personal taste. A pattern is testable in use. People can say whether a light-filled alcove works, whether an entrance transition is too abrupt, whether a room has the two-sided light Alexander argues living rooms need. For our families, the pattern is the natural unit of work: Book XIII's Screen Recipes in the Lawbook are patterns in Alexander's sense, whether or not they use his name — a named situation, a set of forces, a composition that resolves them, and tests that tell you whether the resolution held.

### Image, legibility, and wayfinding: Kevin Lynch

Kevin Lynch's *The Image of the City* (1960), drawn from studies of Boston, Jersey City, and Los Angeles, asked a question architecture had rarely asked empirically: what does a place look like inside the mind of the person moving through it? His answer — that people build mental maps from five kinds of element — remains the working vocabulary of wayfinding design. **Paths** are the channels of movement. **Edges** are boundaries and breaks in continuity. **Districts** are areas with a recognisable common character that one enters and leaves. **Nodes** are strategic focal points — junctions, squares, concentrations of activity — that one enters and from which decisions are made. **Landmarks** are external points of reference, memorable and unique, that one orients by but does not enter.

Lynch called the quality that makes such a map possible *imageability*, or legibility: the degree to which a place can be read, organised into a coherent pattern, and remembered. A legible city is not a prettier city. It is a city in which a stranger makes fewer errors, feels less low-grade anxiety, and can improvise a route when the planned one fails. Wayfinding design, as it developed after Lynch — in hospitals, airports, transit systems, and eventually in software navigation — is applied legibility: signage, sightlines, consistent naming, and spatial structure working together so that orientation is carried by the place itself first and by labels second.

### Modernism, and what it got right and wrong

No honest account of the field can skip the modern movement, because both its gifts and its failures shape every screen we now draw. Modernist architecture — Le Corbusier's *Toward an Architecture* (1923) and his Five Points, the Bauhaus, Mies van der Rohe's pursuit of a universal, flexible space — gave the field the open plan, the honest expression of frame construction, the free façade, and a serious attempt to design for industrial production and public health: light, air, hygiene, minimum dwelling standards. Its central compositional gift to us is the idea that a structural grid can liberate a plan rather than imprison it — the frame carries the loads, so the partitions, and the life between them, can move.

Its failure is equally instructive and is named in the Lawbook's list of failure modes. Too much modernist building — and far too much modern software built in its image — pursued a universal space so neutral that it carried no identity, no district character, no landmark, no graduated hierarchy between public and private. The screenshot, or the rendering, was beautiful; the lived place was illegible. Alexander's whole career, and Lynch's research, can be read as evidence-based replies: human beings do not orient by neutrality. They orient by difference, rhythm, centre, and edge. Cyber-Viking design takes modernism's grid and honesty and refuses its amnesia.

### Environmental psychology: how space acts on people

Environmental psychology, the study of how physical settings affect behaviour and wellbeing, gives the field its evidence about effects that builders had long known by craft. A few of its durable findings matter directly to composition. Prospect and refuge — a preference, documented and debated since Jay Appleton's *The Experience of Landscape* (1975), for settings where one can see out from a position of shelter — explains why the bench along the longhouse wall, back protected, facing the fire and the room, is a seat people choose. Research on crowding, noise, and personal space shows that density is psychological as much as physical: perceived control over one's setting — a door that closes, a light that dims, a corner that is one's own — buffers stresses that raw square metres do not. Work on restorative environments, associated with Rachel and Stephen Kaplan, finds that settings which offer coherence, legibility, complexity held within order, and a degree of mystery — the promise of more to see around the bend — restore depleted attention better than either chaos or monotony.

I cite these as directions of evidence, not magic numbers. Their common thread is this tome's foundation: **spatial order is not decoration applied to function. Spatial order is one of the functions.** A room — or a screen — that cannot be read taxes every task inside it, in attention, error, and fatigue.


## 2. Methods and Measures

Architecture becomes a discipline through methods that can be taught, repeated, and judged. The families need those methods in working form, because a recipe defended only by "it feels right" will lose every argument with a deadline.

**The programme, or brief.** Professional work begins with a programme: an explicit statement of what activities the place must hold, for how many people, in what relationships, with what adjacencies and separations. A good programme names flows as well as rooms — what arrives, what waits, what is stored, what must never meet. In interface terms the programme is the task model: the jobs a hall must hold, their frequency, and their required neighbours. A composition that begins before its programme is known is decoration looking for a problem, and it will find the wrong one.

**Circulation.** Architects draw circulation diagrams — the routes people actually take, their widths, their crossings, their dead ends — and judge a plan heavily by them. Good circulation is short where trips are frequent, legible at every decision point, and graded: public routes broad and obvious, service routes discreet, private routes protected. Bottlenecks, backtracking, and routes that force strangers through working areas are defects measurable on the drawing, before anything is built. The digital equivalent is exact: navigation depth, the number of decisions between a user and their destination, and whether the route can be described afterwards from memory.

**Proportion systems.** From classical orders to Le Corbusier's Modulor (1948), architects have used proportion systems to make sizes across a building belong to one another — column to bay to room to façade — so that the eye, moving between scales, finds kinship rather than accident. The value of a system is not mystical. It is coordination: dimensions chosen from a shared set are easier to build, easier to extend, and easier to perceive as a coherent whole. The Lawbook's spacing scale (a 4 px base, panel padding of 24–32 px, section separation of 48–96 px) and its token discipline are a proportion system in this exact sense, and they should be defended as one — not as numerology, but as the shared ruler that lets twelve screens by four families read as one civilisation.

**Light.** Daylighting is a measurable practice: orientation, opening size and placement, depth of plan relative to window height, glare control, and the deliberate modelling of brightness gradients across a room. Experienced designers compose with brightness the way they compose with walls — a brighter terminus draws movement, a dimmer side chamber invites lingering, even illumination suits inspection and work. Artificial lighting extends the same composition after dark, in layers: ambient, task, and accent. Any reader who has watched a user squint at a low-contrast dashboard in a bright room knows that light composition did not stop mattering when the room became a screen; it changed medium.

**Structure and bay rhythm.** In framed buildings, the bay — the repeating structural interval — is the plan's metre. Setting out a regular bay rhythm, and then deliberately breaking it only at significant places (an entrance, a high seat, a stair), gives occupants a subconscious ruler for where they are. Irregular structure everywhere reads as noise; irregularity nowhere reads as monotony; irregularity at the meaningful point reads as emphasis. This is, note, a judgement that can be taught and checked, and it transfers to grid columns, card rhythms, and rail widths without alteration.

**Judgement: post-occupancy and the critique.** Architecture judges its work after occupation, not only at the handover photograph. Post-occupancy evaluation — observing how people actually use, adapt, avoid, and complain about a place — is the field's standing rebuke to screenshot bias. The crit, the structured studio review, is its teaching form: work is pinned up, walked through in sequence, and defended against named criteria in front of peers. Both practices have direct heirs in our gauntlet: the Book XIV removal tests, the long-content and failure-state tests, and the Real-Use Review are post-occupancy thinking applied before release, which is the only time it is cheap.

**Measures the field actually uses.** Architects work to quantified standards: clear widths for accessibility, travel distances to exits, daylight factors, occupancy loads, and energy performance. Standards vary by jurisdiction and era, and this tome invents none of them. The point is methodological: where a quality can be measured, the field measures it and writes the threshold down; where it cannot, it names a test rather than pretending the quality does not exist. That honesty is the model for Section 4.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through the Lawbook, architecture stops being a source of motifs and becomes what Book I says the whole civilisation must be: structure before ornament. The Universal Law — every themed element must have a measurable job; reduce ornament, glow, texture, motion, or symbolic density before ever touching the information architecture — is, stated in architectural language, simply the demand that a building work as a building before it is admired as a picture. Architects have a word for the opposite failure, and so do we: theme substitution. A stave church is not Norse because a dragon sits on its gable. It is Norse because of how its staves stand, how its tiers shed water, how its plan gathers a congregation around a centre. Remove the dragon and the building remains itself. That is the No-Rune Test, passed in timber eight centuries before we named it.

**Book II — Longhouse Composition and Ship-Built Composition** are the architectural heart of the doctrine, and they divide compositional labour cleanly. Longhouse Composition governs inhabited, lingering space: one elongated order, a dominant centre, graduated hierarchy along an axis, rhythmic structural members, benches — secondary, human-scaled elements — along the edges. Ship-Built Composition governs directed, purposeful space: a keel-axis, economy of parts, overlapping construction, an unmistakable forward. A hall where people dwell and talk wants the first. A flow where people travel with intent — onboarding, checkout, a guided repair — wants the second. Confusing them produces the familiar failures: a chat screen built like a torpedo, all thrust and no hearth; or a three-step form built like a mead hall, in which the traveller is invited to linger at every bench and never arrives.

**Book V — rails, masts, and furniture vocabulary** is architecture's plan discipline stated for screens. The left navigation rail, right context rail, top mast, and bottom command bar are a building's furniture in the architectural sense: the fixed elements that let the variable life of the room arrange itself. Book V's named disciplines translate directly. **Primary Workspace Calm** is the longhouse hearth-law inverted and completed: in the hall, the centre held the fire and therefore the attention; in a working screen, the centre holds the task, and the theme — like the hall's carving — gathers at the edges, the rails, the mast, the thresholds. The **Three-Border Limit** is bay discipline: three frame strengths, systematically repeated, so that containment can be read at a glance and no card invents its own architecture. The grid — 12 columns on desktop, 16 on ultrawide, outer margins of 32–72 px, section gaps of 32–96 px — is the proportion system, the shared ruler discussed in Section 2.

**Book XIII — screen recipes as spatial halls** completes the translation. The Lawbook names recipes — Landing Page, AI Command Center, Samtal Hall, Agent Profile, Knowledge Vault, and the rest — and a recipe,architecturally, is a building type: a known programme given a known spatial resolution, adaptable in detail, recognisable in structure. Lynch supplies the test for every recipe. A good screen, like a legible district, offers paths that can be followed, edges that tell you where one region ends, districts with consistent internal character, nodes where decisions concentrate, and at least one landmark by which the whole can be remembered and described to another person. "Turn left at the hearth" is a direction a longhouse gives for free. Our screens must give their equivalents deliberately.

**What Cyber-Viking practice changes versus the mainstream.** Mainstream interface composition, as commonly shipped, is a stack of cards on an infinite scroll: no axis, no graduated hierarchy, no threshold, no terminus — districts without edges, paths without nodes. It inherits modernism's neutral grid without modernism's structural honesty, and it compensates with surface styling, which is why so much of it collapses under the removal tests. Cyber-Viking practice, read architecturally, changes four things. First, it restores the **centre**: every screen has one dominant focal idea, as the hall has one fire. Second, it restores **graduation**: importance falls off by degree from that centre, instead of every element shouting at equal luminance. Third, it restores **thresholds**: entrances, doors, and transitions are designed moments — Samtal's join door, an agent's profile hero — because how a place is entered determines how it is understood. Fourth, it restores **structural rhythm**: rails, bays, and frame strengths repeat until they can be felt, and break only where meaning requires it.

**Samtal applications.** Samtal is the standing proof, and Book VII states its law in one sentence: *the message stream is the hearth; strong theme belongs mostly in the shell around it.* That is longhouse composition applied without a single decorative timber. The message column is the central nave — calm, reading-width, fire-lit in the sense that all attention gathers there. The hall and channel navigation is the side aisle and the bench row: necessary, human, kept at the edge. Profile heroes, the join door, the Masters' imagery placed in identity zones — these are the carved portals and gable ends, the places where the building is permitted, and expected, to be splendid. The composition also dictates what Samtal must refuse: no ornament behind working text, no ceremonial motion spent on ordinary messages, no district — a new feature, a games surface, a vault — admitted without an edge and a path that Lynch's stranger could describe. When a proposed feature cannot say where it sits in the hall, Rannveig's answer is that the hall has not yet been designed; only a room has been drawn and floated near it.

A last word from the support family. Family IV reminds this tome that buildings are also remembered as images — Borgund against its valley, a longship's prow against weather. The Lawbook budgets for it: one signature moment per screen, ceremony kept scarce. Memorability is a property of structure first — silhouette, axis, centre — and imagery second. Get the plan right and the Arts have something worth painting.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

Each rule below is stated so that a reviewer can test it and record a pass or a failure. "Hall" means any screen, surface, or flow under review.

1. Every hall SHALL declare, in its design record, one dominant focal centre, and every other element SHALL be describable by its graduated distance — functional and visual — from that centre.
2. No hall SHALL present two or more elements at equal maximum visual intensity competing as centres. Where a review finds a second centre, the design SHALL be revised before it is decorated.
3. Every hall SHALL be classifiable as Longhouse Composition (dwelling, lingering, gathering) or Ship-Built Composition (directed travel toward a goal), and its layout SHALL follow the logic of its declared type.
4. Navigation in every hall SHALL satisfy a Lynch audit: its paths, edges, districts, nodes, and at least one landmark SHALL be nameable by a reviewer who did not design it, after a single traversal.
5. A first-time user SHALL be able to state the primary task and primary action of a hall within roughly three seconds of seeing it, per the Universal acceptance battery; failure SHALL block release under Book XIV.
6. Working content — message streams, editors, tables, reading text — SHALL sit in the calm centre. Ornament, background art, and texture SHALL NOT be placed behind working content at any density.
7. Theme and identity density SHALL be concentrated at thresholds and identity zones — entrances, doors, profile heroes, mast, and rails — following the stave-church distribution of carving at portals rather than across wall fields.
8. Every hall SHALL use no more than three visible frame strengths, and corner geometry SHALL follow the Lawbook system (6–12 px cuts on standard controls) without local improvisation.
9. Layout dimensions SHALL be taken from the shared proportion system — the 4 px spacing base, the Appendix A defaults, and Appendix B tokens. A design containing magic values SHALL be returned to its maker, per Hallveig's token governance.
10. Structural rhythm SHALL be regular by default. A break in grid, bay, or rail rhythm SHALL occur only at a point of declared meaning (entry, primary action, state change), and the design record SHALL name that meaning.
11. Circulation SHALL be audited before styling: the reviewer SHALL trace the three most frequent journeys in the programme and record their decision points. Any journey requiring backtracking through a working area SHALL be redesigned.
12. Every district admitted to a product SHALL have a legible edge and a path connecting it to a node. A feature that cannot state its district, edge, and path SHALL NOT be shipped as a floating surface.
13. Ceremonial composition — signature moments, ceremonial motion in the 450–800 ms band, hero imagery — SHALL be reserved for rare, named states. Routine states SHALL NOT receive ceremonial treatment.
14. Every hall SHALL pass the removal battery in order: Grayscale, No-Rune, No-Glow, No-Art, and Three-Second Silhouette. A hall whose hierarchy depends on runes, glow, or artwork SHALL fail, regardless of its appearance with effects on.
15. Every hall SHALL be reviewed with long, empty, and failure-state content before release. A composition proven only on ideal content SHALL be recorded as unproven.
16. New building types SHALL enter the library as named recipes in Book XIII's sense — programme, forces, composition, tests — following Alexander's pattern form. One-off screens that solve a recurring programme SHALL be generalised into a recipe or removed.
17. Family IV imagery SHALL be commissioned against an approved composition, never used to repair one. If a surface needs art to become legible, the composition SHALL be sent back to Family I.
18. Tove's accessibility veto SHALL apply to spatial decisions as to surface ones: a composition that fails at 125–200% text scaling, in a compact viewport, or under reduced motion SHALL NOT ship on the argument that it works at full size with effects on.
