# Art — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 05

*Great Research Campaign · 2026-10-22 · Lead: Family IV — The Arts · Doctrine: Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook*

We are the Arts. Heiðr looks first for the face — who is present, what their silhouette says before a word is read. Védís looks past the face to the weather behind it — the fjord, the hall, the light that makes a place remembered rather than merely recognised. Between us we hold one conviction: a picture is not decoration applied to a finished product. A picture is a decision about what matters, made visible. Every civilisation that has lasted has known this. Every interface that forgets it becomes, in the Lawbook's exact warning, a generic app with symbols pasted on — competent, forgettable, and dead on arrival as a world.

This tome treats art as a design foundation: the history of picture-making, the principles that survive across media, and the discipline of judging an image honestly — the Arts family's working inheritance, read through the doctrine we are bound to serve.

## 1. The Field Itself

Art, in the broad sense used here, is the deliberate making of images and objects that carry meaning beyond their immediate use. That definition matters because it refuses two common reductions. Art is not only painting on canvas in a gallery, and it is not only self-expression. Across most of human history, most art was made for something — a tomb, a temple, a book, a ship, a hall — and its power came precisely from serving that purpose beautifully enough to be remembered. The Cyber-Viking designer who treats art as an optional finish has already misunderstood the field. The historical norm is art *inside* function; the modern separation of the two is the exception, and a recent one.

### Composition: the oldest technology

Beneath every school and style lies composition — the arrangement of forms within a bounded field so that a viewer knows, in order, where to look, what is important, and how the parts relate. Compositional knowledge accumulated slowly and was taught explicitly for centuries. The picture plane is organised by a horizon and by divisions of the field; forms are grouped so the eye travels a designed path rather than wandering; a dominant mass is balanced, often asymmetrically, by smaller counterweights; figures and objects overlap to create depth; and light is distributed unevenly, because even illumination flattens both space and importance.

Classical and Renaissance practice codified much of this. Linear perspective, systematised in fifteenth-century Florence and described by Leon Battista Alberti in *De pictura* (1435), gave painters a rational construction for receding space. Chiaroscuro — the modelling of form through graduated light and dark, brought to a dramatic extreme by Caravaggio and Rembrandt — gave them a construction for attention: what is lit is what is meant. Neither device was a trick. Each solved a real problem: how to make a flat surface hold a believable, legible world.

Two durable principles emerge that no later revolution abolished. First, *hierarchy is composition*: in any durable picture, one thing dominates, a few things support, and everything else quiets down. Second, *the edge matters as much as the centre*: how a form is cropped by the frame declares whether the world continues beyond the picture or is contained by it. Both principles translate directly into interface imagery, as §3 will show.

### The Romantic sublime and Caspar David Friedrich

In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, European art turned from ideal beauty toward the sublime — the experience, theorised by Edmund Burke and Immanuel Kant in different terms, of confronting something vastly greater than the self: mountains, storms, ice, night, ruin, and the sea. Romantic landscape painting made that confrontation its subject, and no painter defines the mode more completely than Caspar David Friedrich (1774–1840).

Friedrich's *Wanderer above the Sea of Fog* (c. 1818, Hamburger Kunsthalle) is the canonical exemplar. A single figure stands on a rocky outcrop, back turned to the viewer, looking over a sea of mist from which ridges and peaks emerge into indefinite distance. The device of the back-turned figure — the *Rückenfigur* — is Friedrich's signature contribution to picture-making. Denied the figure's face and expression, the viewer is placed *inside* the figure's position: we do not watch someone contemplate the world; we contemplate it with him. The composition enforces this. The dark foreground mass anchors the bottom of the picture, the figure's silhouette sits near its centre and vertical axis, and the pale, layered distance opens above and beyond him. Scale is the meaning: one human, upright and still, against immensity that does not notice him.

Friedrich's wider body of work — *The Sea of Ice* (1823–24), *Chalk Cliffs on Rügen* (1818), the moonlit contemplation pictures — repeats a grammar the Arts family studies closely: a restrained palette, a high proportion of sky and atmosphere, figures kept small or turned away so that place, not personality, carries the emotion, and a stillness that invites duration rather than a glance. He is reported to have said the artist should paint not only what is in front of him but what he sees within himself. The lesson for a design practice is not "paint fog." It is that *restraint plus scale plus a single placed figure can carry more feeling than any amount of detail*, and that atmosphere is a compositional material, not a filter applied afterwards.

The sublime also carries a warning. Romantic imagery is powerful precisely because it overwhelms; used carelessly it overwhelms the task it was meant to serve. That tension — between the image that transports and the surface that must remain usable — is the central negotiation of Book IX of the Lawbook, and of this family's daily work.

### The Golden Age of illustration

Between roughly the 1880s and the 1920s, improvements in printing — halftone and colour reproduction that could finally do justice to painted originals — produced what is now called the Golden Age of Illustration. For the first time, superb pictures reached mass audiences inside books and magazines, and a generation of illustrators became, in effect, the visual imagination of their culture.

Howard Pyle (1853–1911), often called the father of American illustration, founded a school at Wilmington, Delaware, and taught a doctrine of picture-making that remains startlingly practical. The illustrator must, in Pyle's teaching, put himself inside the scene — think of himself as an actor in it — and then compose so the viewer is put inside it too: strong diagonals to throw the eye into the action, deliberate placement of a riveting accent colour, and a ruthless selection of the one moment in a story that implies what came before and what comes next. His student N. C. Wyeth (1882–1945) carried that doctrine to its most famous early triumph in the illustrations for *Treasure Island* (1911), images so decisive that they fixed the popular appearance of the pirate for a century.

In Britain, Arthur Rackham (1867–1939) built the opposite pole of the same age: ink line of extraordinary intricacy, muted washes, gnarled trees that behave like characters, and a world in which the enchanted and the unsettling share a border. His editions of *Rip Van Winkle* (1905), *Peter Pan in Kensington Gardens* (1906), and the Grimms' tales showed that illustration could be atmospheric literature in its own right, not a summary of the text beside it. Edmund Dulac and Kay Nielsen extended the age toward the decorative and the stylised, drawing on Art Nouveau and non-European art.

Three lessons from the Golden Age belong in any designer's foundation. First, *illustration is interpretation*: the same text yields a Wyeth or a Rackham, and the difference is a worldview, not a skill gap. A house style is therefore a real decision, not an accident of hiring. Second, *the picture must survive reproduction*: Golden Age artists composed for the printed page they would actually occupy — its size, its paper, its distance from the reader. Designing a hero image without knowing its crop, its overlay, and its screen is the modern version of ignoring the page. Third, *images fix cultural memory*: Wyeth's pirates and Rackham's fairies outlasted the editions they were made for. Interfaces, too, will be remembered by a handful of images. It is worth choosing those images as if they will be the memory — because they will.

### Cinematic art direction

Cinema inherited both traditions — Romantic scale and Golden Age narrative clarity — and added time, camera movement, and production design: the construction of a whole visible world consistent across thousands of shots. The art director's discipline is the closest analogue in any medium to what the Arts family does for a digital hall. A film's world is held together by a controlled palette, recurring materials and silhouettes, a lighting logic that stays stable even as scenes change, and a strict economy in which the sets the camera lingers on are rich while the spaces it passes through stay quiet.

Cinema also formalised what painters knew intuitively about the shot. The establishing shot orients; the close-up confers importance; the direction a figure faces or moves creates expectation about what lies off-screen. A crop that cuts against movement feels wrong; one that leaves space ahead of a gaze feels right. Book IX's rule that a hero crop preserves silhouette and action direction is film grammar written into interface law.

The production-design lesson is the deepest: *consistency is what makes a world believable*. A single spectacular image among unrelated imagery makes a poster taped to a wall, not a world. Worlds are made by repetition with variation — same materials, same light, same frame geometry — sustained until the viewer stops noticing style and starts inhabiting the place.

### Art as cultural memory

Finally, art is how cultures remember themselves. Picture stones, altarpieces, portraits, monuments, and illustrated books are not luxuries bought after a civilisation's real work is done; they are a large part of how its values, its dead, and its warnings survive at all. To choose a hall's hero image, its agent portraits, and its ceremonial scenes is to decide what the hall says about itself when no one is explaining. Copying another culture's remembered images without understanding them is therefore a double failure — aesthetically hollow and ethically careless. The Cyber-Viking answer, developed in the companion tome on Viking art, is not to avoid the past but to study it accurately enough to build forward from it.


## 2. Methods and Measures

Art is often spoken of as if it could not be judged — as if "I like it" ended the conversation. The working field knows better. Artists, critics, conservators, and art historians have developed real methods for making, analysing, and preserving pictures, and a design family that cannot use them is left with taste as its only instrument. Taste is necessary. It is not sufficient.

### Critique: the disciplined conversation

The critique — the *crit* of the art school and studio — is the field's oldest quality method. A work is placed before informed viewers who must do three things in order: describe what is actually there before interpreting it, analyse how its formal means produce its effects, and only then judge how well it achieves what it attempts. The order is the discipline. Most bad feedback, in studios and in product reviews alike, fails by jumping to judgment ("it feels off") without the description and analysis that would locate the fault and make it fixable.

A useful critique vocabulary is formal and specific: focal point, visual weight, balance, rhythm, contrast, value structure, colour temperature, edge quality, negative space, scale, and point of view. Each term names something that can be pointed to and changed. "Make it pop" names nothing; "the portrait and the banner behind it share the same value, so the silhouette disappears" names a fault and implies its cure.

### Composition analysis

Composition analysis is critique applied systematically to arrangement. Its standard operations include: identifying the primary focal area and the path the eye takes to and from it; mapping the value structure by reducing the image to a few tones — a picture that reads clearly in three values will read at any size, and one that does not, will not; checking the silhouette of the principal subject against its ground; locating the horizon and the implied eye level; and examining the crop — what the frame includes, what it cuts, and whether the cuts look intentional or accidental.

Two quick reductions deserve to be standing practice because they expose faults that full-colour, full-size viewing hides. The *thumbnail test*: shrink the image until it is a postage stamp; if the subject and its action cannot be identified, the composition is too weak for the small surfaces — tiles, list rows, notifications — where interface images actually live. The *squint or blur test*: blur the image until detail vanishes; what remains should be a clear arrangement of one dominant mass, supporting masses, and quiet ground. If what remains is uniform speckle, the image has detail without structure — the most common fault in generated and in heavily textured imagery alike.

### Judging across the field: originality, craft, and fit

Mature judgment in art weighs several distinct qualities that are often confused. *Craft* is command of the medium — drawing, colour, and finish that do what the maker intended. *Originality* is the presence of a genuine point of view, as distinct from novelty, which is merely difference. *Expressive force* is whether the work actually produces its intended feeling in a viewer who was not briefed. *Fit* is whether the work serves the purpose and place it was made for — a masterpiece of private symbolism can still be a failure as a book cover, a portrait, or a hero image, because those are public instruments. Professional practice judges all four and refuses to let excellence in one excuse failure in another. This tome's rules apply the same refusal to interface art: a beautiful image in the wrong place, at the wrong density, under unreadable text, is a failed image *for this hall*, whatever its merits elsewhere.

### Conservation of authenticity

Conservation keeps works materially and honestly themselves over time. Its principles — minimal intervention, reversibility where possible, full documentation, and a bright line between preserving a work and improving or modernising it — amount to stewardship: the work is not yours to remake; it is yours to hand on intact, with its history legible.

In digital practice this governs originals and masters. Source artworks are kept unaltered; edits are made on copies; crops and overlays are recorded rather than silently baked into the only surviving file; authorship and date travel with the work. The Arts family adds the user's standing law, stricter than most field practice: every picture carries a true description as alt text, embedded as data inside the file, so the image remains identified wherever it travels. An unidentified image in the archive is, in conservation terms, a work already partly lost.

### The artist's own methods

Practice has its own rigour. Picture-makers work from studies — thumbnails, value sketches, colour studies — before committing to the full work, because composition is cheaper to fix at three centimetres than at three feet. They work under constraint deliberately — a limited palette, a fixed format, a single light source — because constraint forces decision and decision produces character. They step back, because a picture is read at a distance different from the distance at which it is made. And they keep a coherent body of work, because a recognisable world is worth more than a set of impressive accidents. Each habit has an exact counterpart in §4.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

The Lawbook does not ask art to be less artful. It asks art to take its proper, powerful place — and only that place. Book IX (Imagery, Illustration & 3D) is this family's home book, but its doctrine only makes sense inside the Universal Law and beside Books II, III, IV, and XIV.

### The Universal Law applied to images

Every themed element must have a measurable job. For an image, the jobs are enumerable: to identify a place or a person, to establish the emotional register of a surface, to mark a rare ceremonial moment, to make an abstract capability graspable, or to carry memory — the image by which a hall, an agent, or an event will be recalled. An image with no job on that list is not neutral; it competes for attention with everything that does have one. The reduction order follows directly: when an image fights the task — text over it, controls beside it, a decision beneath it — the image yields first. Reduce its brightness, its detail, its area, or remove it, *before* touching the information architecture it was disturbing. And the coherence conditions bind images hardest of all design elements: a surface must remain usable with its art disabled entirely, which means no essential information may exist only inside a picture.

### Book IX: density, crop, and integration

Book IX's central target is the one this family quotes daily: **art density is concentrated in identity zones, not spread over working surfaces.** Identity zones are the places where a person or place declares itself — profile heroes, agent portrait cards, hall doors, gallery surfaces, landing and ceremonial screens. Working surfaces — the message stream, the composer, settings, tables — stay calm. This is not a compromise between art and usability; it is how both traditions of §1 worked. The Golden Age illustrator did not draw over the text block; the cathedral did not carve the reading desk as densely as the portal. Density is meaning: where the art is thickest is where the culture says identity lives.

Book IX's cropping law — **a hero crop preserves the subject's silhouette and action direction** — is film and Friedrich grammar codified. A portrait crop that shears a figure's gesture, a world image that leaves no space in the direction a ship travels or a gaze points, fails even if every pixel in it is beautiful. The companion law on overlays completes it: where text rides over an image, the overlay must be strong enough to guarantee text contrast, always, not on average across the image. Heiðr's portraits and Védís's worlds both pass under Tove's contrast gate before they ship; that pairing is doctrine, not courtesy.

Book IX's authenticity chapter adds the sentence this family treats as sacred: historical motifs should respect actual Viking Age form when representing history, and stylisation must not collapse into horned-helmet fantasy shorthand. And its adult-character chapter states plainly that human characters intended as adult identities must read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context — a law that governs agent portraiture directly, since an agent's portrait is an identity document, not an illustration of a mood.

### Book II: structure beneath the image

Book II (Visual Grammar & Geometry) governs what images sit *inside*. Knotwork and dragon-weave are structural geometry — boundaries and joins — not surface pattern to be poured over a picture; runes, in Book II's system, are nodes on circuit lines, points of connection, not lettering scattered for flavour. For the Arts this means a frame is designed with the same geometry whether or not an image fills it: corner cuts of 6–12 px, at most three visible frame strengths, runestone silhouettes for portrait and card containers. If the frame system is sound, a plain surface and a painted one belong to the same civilisation. If it is not, no painting can rescue it — which is exactly the No-Art removal test arriving early.

### Book III: light spent like gold

Book III (Color, Light & Material) sets the budgets the image-world must live within: all saturated and glowing colour together covers 5–12% of the visible area; depth comes from a ladder of at least four dark surface values, not from colour; glow, where it appears, is built in three parts — core, halo, atmosphere — never as a flat blur. Védís's landscapes are painted to this budget from the first sketch: a world image whose sky burns brighter than the hall's primary action has committed hierarchy inversion, the failure mode in which ornament outshines the task. The correct cinematic instinct helps here — in production design, the world is lit to serve the scene, and the scene in an interface is the user's work.

### Book IV: the rune doctrine, for image-makers

Book IV's rune doctrine binds anyone who puts a mark inside a picture: Elder Futhark and historical rune forms are intentional semantic marks, never a replacement alphabet for ordinary copy, and form, period, transliteration, and intended symbolic role must be verified *before* a rune becomes an icon — or a carving in a hero image, or a device on an agent's portrait. A rune painted into artwork is still a rune; the verification duty does not dissolve because the medium is pictorial. Rúnhildr reviews this family's imagery for exactly this, and her block is absolute.

### The alt & data law

Standing law from the user, aligned with Book XI: every picture carries a detailed alt description — a true spoken account of what the image shows — and that description is embedded as data in the file itself. For the Arts family this is not an accessibility afterthought; it is part of the definition of a finished picture. A portrait is not done when it is beautiful. It is done when it is beautiful, correctly cropped, inside budget, and described truly enough that a person who cannot see it still knows exactly who is present and what the image claims. §1's account of art as cultural memory sharpens the point: the description *is* the memory, in the form that survives every change of screen and sight.

### The No-Cosplay Principle and the removal tests

Book I's No-Cosplay Principle is the Arts family's standing examination of conscience. A design fails it when the Viking quality lives only in detachable costume — a horned helmet here, a faux-rune font there, fur and axes applied to an otherwise generic fantasy surface. The Book XIV removal tests operationalise the principle. Take the runes away: is the world still recognisably itself, through structure, materials, and light? Take the glow away: does the hierarchy stand? Take the art away entirely: does the surface still work, and does it still feel like the same hall, plainer but unbroken? A Védís landscape that passes these tests has done its job properly — it deepened a world that exists independently of it. One that fails them was carrying weight it should never have been asked to bear, and the repair is structural, not pictorial: send it back to Family I, do not paint harder.

### What changes versus mainstream practice, and Samtal

Mainstream product practice treats imagery as content — stock photography chosen late, illustration systems built for cheerful abstraction, hero sections designed as marketing. Cyber-Viking practice differs in four ways. First, imagery is *identity infrastructure*, commissioned and governed like the token system, not selected from a library at the end. Second, it is *concentrated*, not distributed: a few governed identity zones carry the full artistic weight, which lets each image be made properly instead of a hundred surfaces being decorated thinly. Third, it is *historically literate*: its Viking material is checked against the record, as the companion tome demands. Fourth, it is *described*: alt and embedded data are part of the artwork's completion.

In Samtal the applications are concrete and already partly lived. The message stream is the hearth — Book VII's sentence — and stays calm and dark; the art lives in the shell around it: the join door, the landing, the hall's identity panels, agent portraits and profile heroes, the gallery of the hall's remembered moments. Agent portraits follow Book VIII: beautiful, mystical, highly stylised — and identity-consistent across every surface, because a citizen recognised in the hall must be the same citizen in the member list, the mention, and the profile. Ceremonial images — the rare, full-dress scenes — are reserved for moments that deserve memory, in Book I's phrase, and are never spent on routine states. When a new master image enters Samtal, this family's test is the one §2 taught: describe it, reduce it to values, shrink it to a thumbnail, then remove it and check the hall still stands. Only then is it art *for this hall*.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

Each rule below is stated so it can be tested, and each traces to the argument of §1–3. "Image" means any picture, portrait, illustration, render, or photograph placed by any family.

1. Every image SHALL carry a named job — identify a person or place, set a surface's register, mark a ceremony, explain a capability, or carry memory — recorded with the asset. An image with no recorded job SHALL NOT ship. (§1, §3)
2. Art density SHALL be concentrated in identity zones — doors, landings, profiles, portrait cards, galleries, ceremonial screens. Working surfaces, including the message stream and composer, SHALL NOT carry intricate art behind or beneath working text. (§3, Book IX)
3. When an image competes with a task, the image SHALL be reduced, dimmed, cropped down, or removed before any change is made to information architecture. (§3, Universal Law)
4. Every surface SHALL remain coherent and fully usable with its art removed, its glow disabled, and in grayscale. No essential information SHALL exist only inside an image. (§3, Book XIV)
5. A hero crop SHALL preserve the principal subject's complete silhouette and SHALL leave space in the direction of the subject's gaze, gesture, or movement. A crop that severs a gesture or reverses action direction SHALL be rejected. (§1, §3, Book IX)
6. Where text rides over an image, contrast SHALL meet WCAG AA at every point under the text, guaranteed by overlay or by placement on a calm area of the image — never by averaging across the picture. (§3, Books IX, XI)
7. Every image SHALL pass the thumbnail test and the three-value test of §2 before acceptance: identifiable subject and action at tile size, and a clear dominant-mass structure when reduced to three tones. Failing images SHALL be recomposed, not enlarged or sharpened. (§2)
8. Every shipped picture SHALL carry a detailed, truthful alt description, and that description SHALL be embedded as data inside the file. An undescribed picture is unfinished work and SHALL NOT be counted as delivered. (§2, §3)
9. Original artworks SHALL be preserved unaltered as masters. Crops, overlays, and treatments SHALL be applied to copies and recorded; no treatment SHALL be silently baked into the only surviving file. Authorship and date SHALL travel with the asset. (§2)
10. A new image SHALL be critiqued in the order of §2 — description, formal analysis, judgment — by a family other than its maker before it ships. Judgment-first feedback SHALL be returned for rework. (§2)
11. Images SHALL be composed for the actual surface they will occupy — its size, crop, overlay, and viewing distance — and SHALL be reviewed in place with real content, never only as standalone files. (§1, §2)
12. Any rune, historical motif, or period detail appearing inside an image SHALL be verified for form, period, and meaning with Rúnhildr before the image ships. Horned helmets and other discredited fantasy shorthand SHALL NOT appear in any image presented as historical. (§3, Books IV, IX)
13. Characters intended as adult identities SHALL read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context in every portrait. Portraits of the same agent SHALL remain identity-consistent across all surfaces. (§3, Books VIII, IX)
14. Saturated and glowing colour across a screen, including its images, SHALL remain within the 5–12% accent budget, and no image SHALL be brighter in visual weight than the screen's primary action. (§3, Book III)
15. Ceremonial, full-dress imagery SHALL be reserved for rare, memorable states. Routine, loading, empty, and error states SHALL NOT be dressed as ceremonies. (§1, §3, Book I)
16. A body of imagery for one hall SHALL share palette, materials, lighting logic, and frame geometry, with variation inside that system. A single spectacular image that breaks the system SHALL be brought into the system or rejected, however strong it is alone. (§1, §3)

## 5. Sources and Honesty Note

**Established knowledge.** §1 and §2 rest on the standard history and practice of Western art as taught and published by major institutions: composition and perspective as described by Leon Battista Alberti in *De pictura* (1435); the sublime as theorised by Edmund Burke, *A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful* (1757), and Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Judgment* (1790); the life and work of Caspar David Friedrich, including *Wanderer above the Sea of Fog* (c. 1818) and *The Sea of Ice* (1823–24), held by the Hamburger Kunsthalle; the Golden Age of Illustration and the careers of Howard Pyle, N. C. Wyeth, and Arthur Rackham, documented by the Delaware Art Museum (holder of a major Pyle collection) and the Brandywine Museum of Art (holder of major Wyeth holdings); and conservation ethics as codified by professional bodies such as the American Institute for Conservation. Specific points consulted during this campaign include published museum and reference summaries of Friedrich's *Rückenfigur* device and of Pyle's teaching on composition, diagonals, and accent colour. No statistics, studies, or quotations beyond the widely attested remark attributed to Friedrich in §1 have been relied upon, and none have been invented.

**Cyber-Viking application.** §3 and §4 are not art history. They are this campaign's application of Volmarr's Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook — its Universal Law, Books I–IV, IX, XI, and XIV, the Appendix B budgets, and the user's standing alt-text and embedded-data law (2026-10-19/22) — to the established material of §1–2, written in the voice of Family IV (Heiðr, Védís, Sólrún). Where §3–4 state a rule, the authority is the Lawbook and the family's judgment, not the historical record. The historical record is used in §3 only for what it genuinely shows: that density, hierarchy, and world-consistency were working principles of illustrators, painters, and production designers long before they were interface law.
