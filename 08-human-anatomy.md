# Human Anatomy — Cyber-Viking Research Tome 08

*Family IV draws people. That sentence is a responsibility before it is a skill, and this tome is written in that order: first the body as it truly is — bone, muscle, weight, variation — and only then the hand that renders it. Our drafting discipline is tempered throughout by Tove of Family II, who asks of every figure the warden's questions: Whose body is this? Does it work the way a body works? ## 1. The Field Itself

Artistic anatomy is the study of the human body's structure for the purpose of representing it convincingly and expressively. It is not medical anatomy, though it borrows medicine's map. The physician learns the body to heal it and names everything; the artist learns the body to *build* it on a flat surface or in a viewport, and so studies selectively — the structures that change the surface, govern movement, or carry expression — and learns them as forms in space, not as lists. A muscle's name matters to the artist only insofar as knowing its attachments explains the shape it makes when it pulls. That selectivity is the field's defining intelligence: anatomy for artists is anatomy organized by visibility, function, and drawing consequence.

The tradition is old and unbroken. Egyptian and early Greek figure art worked from canons — fixed proportional schemes that made figures consistent and legible across a culture's whole output. Classical Greek sculptors of the fifth century BCE, above all Polykleitos, whose lost treatise was itself called the *Canon*, pursued proportion as a philosophy: the figure as a harmony of measured parts, each commensurate with the whole. The Renaissance fused that inheritance with direct investigation. Leonardo da Vinci dissected human cadavers and drew what he found with an engineer's curiosity about levers and loads; Michelangelo's figures are unthinkable without the same study; and the écorché — the flayed figure, skin removed, muscles displayed, posed like a living statue — became the tradition's emblematic teaching object, at once beautiful and faintly terrible. Through the academies of the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, life drawing and anatomical study formed the spine of an artist's education: draw from the living model, study the skeleton and the écorché, copy the masters, repeat for years. The twentieth century democratized the knowledge into books that still anchor teaching today, and the twenty-first has added 3D reference, photographic turnarounds, and digital sculpting — new mirrors, same body.

The canon, as working artists actually use it, rests on a few load-bearing ideas.

**Proportion by head-units.** The most durable measuring system takes the height of the head — crown to chin — as a module and expresses the figure's height and landmarks in multiples of it. An average adult stands roughly seven and a half heads tall; the idealized figures of classical and illustration traditions use eight, which lengthens the legs and grants a heroic calm; fashion figures run taller still, and everyone in the field understands these as dialects, not truths. Within the system, the landmarks are what matter: in the common eight-head scheme, the chin, nipples, navel, and pubis fall near successive head divisions down the front of the body, the pubis sits near the figure's midpoint, the wrists align roughly with the pubic level when the arms hang, the elbows near the navel, and the fingertips reach about mid-thigh. Shorter canons compress; real bodies deviate in every direction. The system's value is diagnostic — it gives the artist a grid against which *this particular body's* proportions become visible as deliberate facts rather than accidents of a drifting pencil.

**The skeleton as architecture.** Beneath every surface form stands the skeleton, and the artist studies it as structure: the skull's two great masses (cranium and face, divided near the brow and cheekbones); the spine's double-S curve, which is the body's central spring and the reason a standing figure is a balanced stack of counter-curves rather than a column; the ribcage as a tapered, tiltable barrel; the pelvis as a solid bowl whose tilt governs the whole pose; and the long bones of the limbs with their visible landmarks — clavicles, shoulder blades, elbows, the bony points of wrist, hip, knee, and ankle — where bone comes to the surface and gives the draughtsman fixed points that do not move when flesh does. Bony landmarks are the artist's surveying stakes: soft tissue varies endlessly, but the skeleton's outcrops locate the figure in space.

**Muscle as form and engine.** Over the skeleton, the muscles the artist must know are those that shape the surface and move the masses: the great movers of the torso (pectorals, abdominals with their segmented front, the obliques, the broad latissimus sweeping the back), the deltoid capping the shoulder, the arm's flexor and extensor groups divided by the biceps and triceps, the gluteals, the quadriceps and hamstrings dividing the thigh front and back, the calves, and — most studied of all after the torso — the small, intricate machinery of the neck and face. The sternocleidomastoid, running from behind the ear to the sternum and clavicle, is the neck's visible rope and the portraitist's constant companion. Facial muscles are thin, layered, and attach into skin itself, which is why expression is a whole-face event and why the planes of the head (below) matter more to likeness than any feature drawn in isolation. Fat and skin complete the system and humble the beginner: fat rounds, softens, gathers by sex, age, and individual pattern, and obeys gravity in folds; a figure drawn as pure musculature reads as an écorché, not a living person.

**Planes and masses.** The constructive tradition reduces the body to a small set of large three-dimensional masses — head as a modified box-and-sphere, ribcage, pelvis, limbs as tapered cylinders and wedges — each with planar faces that catch light predictably. John Asaro's *Planes of the Head* (1976) gave this idea its most famous teaching object for the face; the same thinking scales to the whole figure. Planes are how an artist keeps a figure solid under invented light: if you know which way each plane faces, you know its value, and the figure turns in space instead of flattening into an outline with shading sprinkled on.

**Gesture and weight.** Before any of the above, the living figure has gesture: the single flowing line of action that runs through a pose, the tilt of the head answered by the tilt of the pelvis, the weight settling through one leg while the other rests free. A standing figure balances its masses around a plumb line from the pit of the neck through the supporting foot; break that balance knowingly and the figure moves, falls, dances. Gesture is the field's first principle and its final judge — an anatomically perfect figure with dead gesture is a specimen, while a thirty-second gesture drawing with true weight can be fully alive.

The canonical teachers divide the inheritance by temperament. George Bridgman's *Constructive Anatomy* (1920) and *Bridgman's Complete Guide to Drawing from Life* (1952) teach the body as interlocking wedges and masses in motion — anatomy as engineering, forms twisting on a central axis. Andrew Loomis, in *Figure Drawing for All It's Worth* (1943) and *Drawing the Head and Hands* (1956), teaches ideal proportion, clear planar construction, and the head built from a sphere with its sides planed — anatomy made teachable to the working illustrator. Michael Hampton's *Figure Drawing: Design and Invention* (2009) carries the constructive method into contemporary concept art: gesture first, then simplified volumetric anatomy with landmarks an artist can actually remember and invent from. Around these stand the scholarly atlases — Paul Richer's artistic anatomy from the French academic tradition, and modern references such as Valerie Winslow's *Classic Human Anatomy* (2009) — which the working artist consults the way a writer consults a dictionary.

## 2. Methods and Measures

Artistic anatomy is practiced constructively: the artist does not copy a body's outline and decorate it, but builds the figure from the inside out, in a sequence the whole tradition recognizes. The sequence is the method, and each stage has its own checks and its own characteristic failures.

**Stage one — gesture.** Thirty seconds to two minutes, whole arm, one flowing line of action, then the great tilts: head, ribcage, pelvis, and the weight leg. The check is balance and life: drop a plumb line from the pit of the neck and confirm the figure's support explains itself. The characteristic failure is starting with a head oval and stacking body parts beneath it — the mannequin-pile, stiff before it begins.

**Stage two — construction.** The gesture is given volumes: ribcage and pelvis as solid masses with stated tilts, limbs as tapered forms, head as cranium-and-face construction (Loomis's sphere with planed sides and a jaw mass, or an equivalent scheme), all in perspective, all checked against each other with the head-unit measure and with comparative sighting — the atelier technique of holding a measure at arm's length to compare lengths and angles directly. Sighting is the field's original measuring instrument and still its honesty training: it forces the eye to report the particular body rather than the remembered symbol for one. The characteristic failures of this stage are proportion drift (each part plausible, the whole impossible), symmetrical stiffness (both sides of the body doing the same thing, which living bodies almost never do), and what teachers call lasso-and-fill: a contour invented first and anatomy stuffed inside it, producing figures whose insides could never have made those outsides.

**Stage three — anatomy proper.** Landmarks are placed — clavicles, sternum, the hip points, knees, elbows — and the muscle forms are laid over the construction in their correct attachments and directions, checked for the pose's actual mechanics: a muscle that is contracting is short and full; its antagonist is long and quiet; a limb's silhouette is the sum of these states, not a tube of even width. The checks here are functional. Could this shoulder lift that arm? Does this knee bend where knees bend, with the kneecap riding where it rides? Where does the weight-bearing leg's anatomy compress and the free leg's release? The classic failure modes are a litany every life-room teacher knows: muscles drawn as surface decoration copied from charts (the "anatomy tattoo," where a relaxed body wears a bodybuilder's flexed map); heads too small on heroic bodies and too large on naturalistic ones; hands and feet shrunk or hidden because they are hard — the tradition's standing joke is also its diagnostic, since hands carry nearly the forearm's complexity in a fraction of its size; necks drawn as cylinders with the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius ignored, so the head sits like a ball on a post; and breasts, fat, and age drawn from formula rather than from gravity and observation.

**Stage four — surface, light, and finish.** Planes are resolved into values under a stated light, fat and skin soften the construction without erasing it, and the drawing is checked as a whole: does the figure still stand, breathe, and occupy its ground? Teachers measure success by invention, the field's true examination: a student who has only copied photographs collapses when asked to draw the same figure from another angle, in another light, doing something unphotographed. Constructive knowledge is precisely what survives the removal of reference, which is why Hampton's "design and invention" framing and Bridgman's insistence on form-in-rotation remain the working standard.

**Measures of the body itself** are handled with a care this tome insists on recording. Artists measure proportions in head-units, hand-lengths, and comparative sightings — relative measures, deliberately. Absolute ideals are teaching scaffolds. Competent practice keeps the scaffold visibly labelled as a scaffold: the eight-head figure is *a* canon for heroic illustration, the seven-and-a-half-head figure an observed average with wide real variance around it, and any individual body — tall, short, heavy, slight, old, young, disabled, pregnant, scarred — is data, not deviation. Assessment in serious training is therefore double: accuracy to the model in front of you, and structural truth in the figure you invent. A drawing can honour the first and fail the second (a traced flatness), or claim the second while insulting the first (a "corrected" model, idealized against their will). Neither passes.

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

The Lawbook does not contain a book of anatomy, and that is correct: anatomy is not a style. It is the load-bearing knowledge beneath Book IX (Imagery, Illustration & 3D), and the doctrine treats it the way the doctrine treats all structure — as the part that must still be true when every effect is removed. A Cyber-Viking figure judged by Book XIV faces the No-Art and No-Glow tests like any screen: strip the aura, the rune-light, the cinematic rim, and ask what remains. What remains is a body. If that body cannot stand, reach, turn its head, or be the age it claims to be, the art has failed at the level the Lawbook calls hierarchy inversion — ornament brighter than the task — except that here the task is a person.

Book IX, Ch. 130 states the doctrine this tome serves, in the bespoke sentence the doctrine preserves: human characters intended as adult identities must read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context. That sentence is anatomical before it is anything else. Adult readability is built from proportion (an adult's head is small relative to the body; large-head ratios read young, which is why chibi and child canons work and why they are forbidden shortcuts for adult characters), from facial structure (the adult skull's developed brow, jaw, and mid-face proportions differ measurably from a child's rounder, higher-browed arrangement), and from posture and bearing, which anatomy governs and costume merely underlines. Heiðr's portrait system (Book VIII, Ch. 114) inherits the same law at head-and-shoulders scale: identity consistency across surfaces is impossible if the underlying cranial construction drifts, because likeness *is* proportion held stable.

Book II's silhouette law is anatomy's public face. The Lawbook's first visual law — Silhouette — is, for figures, a direct demand for gesture and mass: a character whose black shape has no line of action, no answering tilts of ribcage and pelvis, no weight, fails the three-second silhouette test for the same reason a gesture drawing fails, and the fix is the life-room's fix, not a filter. This is also where mainstream practice and Cyber-Viking practice part company most visibly. Mainstream commercial figure design too often begins from a house template body — one height, one build, one age, endlessly re-costumed — because templates are fast and brand-safe. The Lawbook's civilization principle forbids the shortcut in both directions: a hall of citizens is not a row of clones (structure is ancient; a longhouse held elders, children grown, the scarred, the broad, the slight), and the tokens-and-system discipline of Book XII is meant to systematize *furniture and light*, never to stamp human beings from one die. Deterministic identity generation, applied to people, means a person's proportions are part of their identity seed — stable across every render — not a default the generator relaxes everyone toward.

Book XI brings Tove's hand into this family's work, and §1–2's respect for real bodies becomes enforceable doctrine here. Book XI's laws (Perception, Input, Comprehension, Resilience, Efficiency, Fallback) govern screens, but its spirit — design for the thousandth user, on the worst day, in the real body they have — is the warden's standing question to every figure we draw. The application is plain. No single ideal is imposed: the canons of §1 are scaffolds with labels, and a design family that ships only eight-head heroic adults has not chosen a style, it has failed an audit. Bodies are drawn as they work: joints that articulate truly, weight that lands, age written in structure (spine, skin, proportion, bearing) rather than in a grey wig on a young figure, disability drawn as the person's actual embodiment — a wheelchair user's shoulders and arms carry the strength their life builds; a prosthesis is engineered form, drawn with the same respect Sólrún gives any artifact — and never as a costume of inspiration laid over a template. Fat, muscle, and bone are observed distributions, not moral categories; the écorché is a teacher in the studio and is never the public standard for how a citizen of the hall should look.

For Samtal, the applications are concrete. Agent and human character art — profile heroes, portrait systems, the figures in hall imagery — is built constructively and checked in the order of §2: gesture, construction, anatomy, finish, with the adult-readability audit applied to every adult character and the silhouette test applied at the sizes the hall actually renders. Generated imagery is reviewed against the same standards as drawn work, because generation's characteristic failures are precisely §2's failure litany at machine speed: hands that cannot hold, joints that bend nowhere, proportions that drift between panels of the same person. The alt & data law closes the loop: a figure's alt text describes the person — build, age presentation, posture, attire — in the same factual, dignified register the description would use if the person were standing in the room, and that description travels inside the file. The hall's people are not content. Anatomy, in this house, is how we keep that sentence true in practice.

## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

1. Every figure SHALL be constructed in order — gesture, volumes, anatomy, surface — and no finished figure SHALL be approved whose gesture and weight cannot be demonstrated in an underdrawing or construction pass.
2. Every standing or walking figure SHALL pass the plumb-line check: its balance SHALL be explicable from its supports, or its motion SHALL be the deliberate subject of the pose.
3. Proportion SHALL be checked in head-units against the figure's intended canon, and the intended canon SHALL be recorded with the work; unrecorded drift SHALL be treated as error.
4. Characters intended as adults SHALL read clearly as adults through face, proportions, styling, posture, and context, in silhouette and at final display size; large-head or otherwise juvenile proportion schemes SHALL NOT be used for adult identities.
5. Joints SHALL articulate at their true locations and ranges; hands and feet SHALL be drawn at true relative size and SHALL NOT be hidden, shrunk, or cropped away to avoid difficulty.
6. Muscle forms SHALL reflect the pose's actual mechanics — contracting groups full, antagonists quiet — and relaxed or ordinary bodies SHALL NOT wear a flexed bodybuilding map.
7. No single body template SHALL serve as the unexamined base for a population; casts, halls, and galleries SHALL show real variance in height, build, proportion, age, and embodiment.
8. Age SHALL be constructed structurally (proportion, spine, bearing, skin), never applied as surface costume over a young body.
9. Disability and bodily difference SHALL be drawn from observation of how that body actually works and carries itself, with the same constructive rigour as any figure; they SHALL NOT be rendered as decoration, metaphor, or inspiration shorthand.
10. Fat and soft tissue SHALL obey gravity and the pose; idealized erasure of a represented person's actual build SHALL NOT be performed in portraiture of real or persistent characters.
11. A persistent character's proportions SHALL be part of their identity record and SHALL remain stable across artists, renders, and generators, under Book XII's determinism.
12. Generated figures SHALL pass the same construction audit as drawn ones, with explicit checks for hands, joint articulation, and cross-image proportion drift before acceptance.
13. Every shipped figure SHALL pass the removal battery in sequence: silhouette legible with all glow, aura, and texture removed; adult and identity readability intact in grayscale.
14. Alt text for figure imagery SHALL describe the person's build, age presentation, posture, and attire factually and with dignity, and SHALL be embedded as data in the file.
