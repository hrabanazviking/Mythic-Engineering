## 1. The Field Itself

Human design in this campaign means **human factors and ergonomics** — the scientific discipline concerned with understanding interactions among humans and other elements of a system, and the profession that applies theory, principles, data, and methods to design in order to optimise human well-being and overall system performance. That wording follows the International Ergonomics Association (IEA), and its two commitments matter equally: performance *and* well-being. A system that is fast because it injures, exhausts, or confuses its operator has not succeeded. It has externalised its cost onto the body.

### A short history with a hard lesson

The word *ergonomics* — from the Greek *ergon*, work, and *nomos*, law — was used by the Polish scholar Wojciech Jastrzębowski in 1857, and reintroduced in 1949 by K. F. H. Murrell and colleagues in Britain, where the Ergonomics Research Society was founded. In North America the parallel term *human factors* took hold. The disciplines are essentially synonymous, with regional and cultural shadings: European ergonomics grew strongly from work physiology and occupational health; American human factors grew strongly from experimental psychology and engineering.

Both were forged in the Second World War. Aircraft, radar, and other new systems produced a pattern engineers initially misread as operator error: skilled, selected, highly motivated people repeatedly made the same mistakes, sometimes fatally. Psychologists such as Paul Fitts and Alphonse Chapanis showed that the pattern lay in the design — controls that felt alike but did opposite things, displays that violated population expectations, layouts that required impossible discrimination under stress. Chapanis’s work on shape-coding aircraft controls — making landing-gear and flap controls tactually distinct — is the field’s founding parable: do not train people to survive a confusing design; redesign so the confusion cannot take hold. The postwar lesson generalised to aviation safety, process control, medicine, and eventually software: when many good people fail the same way, blame the system first.

The field institutionalised quickly — the IEA was founded in 1959 — and broadened steadily. Henry Dreyfuss’s *Designing for People* (1955) and his anthropometric charts brought human measurement into industrial design. Jens Rasmussen on skills, rules, and knowledge, James Reason’s *Human Error* (1990) on organisational accidents, and Don Norman’s translation into everyday design carried the field from cockpits into control rooms, hospitals, and consumer products.

### The three domains

The IEA traditionally distinguishes three domains of specialisation.

**Physical ergonomics** concerns anatomical, anthropometric, physiological, and biomechanical characteristics as they relate to physical activity: posture, reach, force, repetition, lifting, vibration, and the design of workstations and tools. Its core questions are fit and load — does the body fit the artefact, and what does using it cost the body over time?

**Cognitive ergonomics** concerns mental processes — perception, memory, reasoning, decision-making, and motor response — as they affect interaction. Its questions are legibility and load of the mind: can the person perceive the state, understand it, decide, and act, especially under time pressure, interruption, or fatigue?

**Organisational ergonomics** (macroergonomics) concerns sociotechnical systems: shift structures, communication, teamwork, safety culture, and the design of work itself. Interfaces fail inside organisations, not only inside skulls; an alert that is technically perfect and operationally always overridden is a failed design.

A screen-based product lives mainly in the second domain, touches the first through posture, reach, and target design, and answers to the third whenever it mediates a team — which, in a hall like Samtal, is always.

### Anthropometrics: designing for a population, not a person

Anthropometry is the measurement of human body dimensions and physical characteristics — stature, eye height, reach, hand size, grip strength, joint ranges — reported as distributions, conventionally in percentiles. The 5th-percentile woman and the 95th-percentile man are the field’s standard boundary figures, not because anyone is exactly those figures, but because designing for a stated range is an explicit, auditable choice about whom to include.

Three principles govern its use. First, **there is no average human.** A design fitted to the average on ten dimensions fits almost no one on all ten. Design must accommodate ranges dimension by dimension, with adjustability where one fixed value cannot serve the range. Second, **clearance and reach pull in opposite directions.** Clearance must fit the large body (high percentile); reach must serve the small body (low percentile). Confusing the two systematically excludes someone. Third, **data must match the population.** Tables are population- and date-specific. Stephen Pheasant’s *Bodyspace* remains the canonical practical text, and its discipline is humility: know whose bodies your data describe, and whose they do not. For digital interfaces, anthropometrics becomes concrete in fingertip size — wide, occluding what it touches — thumb reach, viewing distance, and users who may be walking, gloved, elderly, or trembling. No visual polish repeals that.

### Perception: the senses are instruments with specifications

Human perception is not a camera feed. It is an active, limited, context-sensitive system, and human design treats its properties as engineering parameters.

**Vision** dominates interface design, so its limits dominate failures. Visual acuity falls sharply away from the fovea; peripheral vision is sensitive to motion and flicker but poor at detail and colour. Contrast sensitivity depends on luminance, size, and age — the lens yellows and the pupil narrows across the lifespan, so older users need more light and more contrast for the same legibility. Dark adaptation is slow and easily destroyed by a single bright screen. Colour vision deficiency affects a substantial minority — roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women for the common red–green forms in populations of Northern European ancestry, with rates varying by ancestry — which is why colour can never be the only channel for essential information (see §3). Gestalt principles — proximity, similarity, common region, closure, continuity, described by Wertheimer, Koffka, and Köhler in the early twentieth century — describe how people group elements before they read them; layout that fights grouping forces serial, effortful inspection.

**Audition** carries alerts and speech, with its own limits: masking (one sound hiding another), age-related high-frequency loss (presbycusis), and the startle cost of loud or meaningless signals. An alarm that means everything is heard as nothing. **Touch** carries confirmation when eyes are busy; haptic feedback succeeds when it is distinct, timed, and reserved — continuous vibration is not feedback, it is weather. **Attention and vigilance** are the scarcest resources of all. Sustained vigilance degrades within tens of minutes — the decrement first studied in wartime radar watchkeeping by Norman Mackworth — and focused attention misses even salient events elsewhere, as inattentional-blindness experiments (Simons and Chabris, 1999) showed. Design that assumes continuous attention assumes a human who does not exist.

### Cognition: load, memory, and decision

**Working memory** — the mental workspace for holding and manipulating information — is severely limited. George Miller’s 1956 paper, “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two,” described limits on absolute judgement and immediate memory in terms of *chunks*; Nelson Cowan’s later synthesis (2001) placed the capacity of the focus of attention closer to four chunks in most adults. Either way, the design consequence is identical: present information in meaningful groups, keep needed context visible, and never require a person to hold an unchunked string — a code, a set of values, a location — in mind while navigating elsewhere.

**Cognitive load theory** (John Sweller and colleagues, from the late 1980s) distinguishes the intrinsic load of a task itself from extraneous load imposed by its presentation, and directs design effort at cutting the second: split attention between mutually referring sources, redundant decoration, and search through undifferentiated material are extraneous load. Every ornamental element behind working text is, in this vocabulary, a load decision — and an indefensible one.

**Long-term memory and skill** change what load means. Experts chunk where novices see fragments — chess masters’ board memory is the classic demonstration — and Rasmussen’s skill–rule–knowledge framework predicts behaviour accordingly: skilled performance is fast and automatic until an unfamiliar situation forces slow, knowledge-based reasoning, exactly when support is most needed and most often absent. Fitts and Posner’s stages of skill acquisition (cognitive, associative, autonomous) complete the picture: interfaces must serve the novice’s reasoning and the expert’s automation simultaneously, because every expert was a novice and every expert becomes a novice again at the new feature, the rare procedure, and the emergency.

**Decision and error.** Human error, in Reason’s taxonomy, divides into slips (the intention was right, the action went wrong — capturing, misordering, mistapping), lapses (memory failures), and mistakes (the plan itself was wrong, from misread state or a wrong mental model). Each class has a different remedy: slips yield to constraints, distinct controls, and feedback; lapses to visible state and reminders; mistakes to better conceptual models and decision support. A field that classifies error this precisely has no use for “user error” as a verdict. It is a starting hypothesis about the design.

**Situation awareness** (Mica Endsley, 1995) — perceiving elements, comprehending their meaning, projecting their future state — names what dashboards and presence systems are for. Automation complicates it: the out-of-the-loop problem shows that passive monitors of reliable automation lose awareness and skill, then are handed control exactly when automation reaches its limit. Human design therefore asks of every agent: what keeps the human in the loop enough to remain its master? **Mental workload**, the experienced cost of a task, degrades performance at both extremes — overload fragments attention, underload invites mind-wandering — and is measurable (§2), therefore budgetable. A hall that adds motion, sound, and badges is spending its members’ workload. The question is whether it knows the price.


## 2. Methods and Measures

Human design is practised as applied science: observe the human in the system, model the demands, measure against criteria, redesign, and verify. Its authority comes from that loop and from standards that encode its findings.

### Analysis methods

**Task analysis** decomposes work into goals, subtasks, decisions, and information needs. Hierarchical task analysis (Annett and Duncan, 1967) remains a foundational technique; cognitive task analysis extends it to expert judgement — the cues experts notice, the decisions they make under uncertainty — using interviews and observation of real incidents. No workload or error claim is meaningful without a task analysis underneath it, because load is always load *for a task*.

**Human reliability and error analysis** — techniques such as SHERPA (Systematic Human Error Reduction and Prediction Approach) and HEART, and Reason’s organisational analysis — systematically walk a task asking where slips, lapses, and mistakes can occur and what would prevent or catch them. In interface practice this survives as error-focused design review: for every consequential action, name the plausible slip, the forcing function or constraint that blocks it, and the recovery if it happens anyway.

**Anthropometric and biomechanical assessment** applies percentile data, reach and clearance envelopes, posture analysis, and — in physical work — tools such as the NIOSH Revised Lifting Equation for manual handling risk. In digital practice its descendants are thumb-reach maps, target-size standards, viewing-distance and text-size calculations, and posture-aware workstation guidance.

**Workload and situation-awareness measurement** uses validated instruments. The **NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX)** (Hart and Staveland, 1988) rates mental, physical, and temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration, and remains the standard subjective workload measure. Situation awareness is assessed by techniques such as **SAGAT** (Endsley), which freezes a simulation and queries the operator’s knowledge of current state. Physiological measures — heart-rate variability, eye tracking, EEG in research — add objective channels, with the caution that such a signal evidences arousal or attention, not usability directly.

**Usability and accessibility evaluation** belong here as much as in Tome 01: task success, time, and errors under ISO 9241-11; heuristic and expert review; and conformance testing against WCAG and platform accessibility requirements. Human design adds the populations mainstream testing omits — older adults, low-vision and motor-impaired users, users under fatigue, glare, motion, and one-handed use — because its founding insight is that the *conditions* are part of the design problem.

### Standards and thresholds that genuinely exist

- **ISO 9241**, *Ergonomics of human-system interaction*, is the governing family: Part 11 (usability), Part 110 (interaction principles), Part 210 (human-centred design), Part 303 and related parts (display requirements), and others. Its European adoption makes parts of it a reference for workplace display-screen obligations; the UK’s Display Screen Equipment Regulations and similar regimes translate ergonomic principles into employer duties.
- **WCAG 2.1 / 2.2 (W3C)** set the testable accessibility thresholds used throughout this campaign: contrast 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text at AA (1.4.3); 3:1 non-text contrast for essential components (1.4.11); reflow at 320 CSS px (1.4.10); text resize to 200% without loss (1.4.4); and, in 2.2, target size minimum 24 × 24 CSS px at AA (2.5.8) with an enhanced 44 × 44 px criterion at AAA (2.5.5). The distance between the AA floor and the AAA figure is itself instructive — see §3.
- **Platform human-interface guidance** — Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines and Google’s Material Design — codify ergonomic findings for practitioners, including touch targets in the 44 pt / 48 dp region respectively and dynamic-type / font-scaling requirements. They are guidance, not law; their convergence near the same numbers is the evidence.
- **ANSI/HFES 100** (*Human Factors Engineering of Computer Workstations*) and the **MIL-STD-1472** lineage in defence procurement show the field’s other register: procurement-grade requirements on displays, controls, labelling, and workspace layout, written because buyers learned that “ergonomic” as an adjective enforces nothing.
- **Section 508** (US), the **European Accessibility Act**, and EN 301 549 incorporate WCAG-based criteria into law and procurement, which is why AA functions in practice as a floor rather than an aspiration.

### How the field judges a design

A human-design judgement asks five questions, in order. *Who* is the population, stated as ranges and abilities, not an average? *What task*, analysed, in what context — posture, light, noise, interruption, duration? *What demands* does the design place on perception, memory, decision, and the body, and how were they measured or modelled? *What errors* are foreseeable, by class, and what prevents, catches, or forgives each? And *what evidence* — test, standard, or instrument, with what sample — supports the claim that demands sit within human limits? A design that cannot answer the first question cannot be rescued by excellence on the rest. Most exclusion is decided there, silently, in the choice of whose body and mind counted as “the user.”

## 3. Read Through the Lawbook

Read through Volmarr’s Cyber-Viking Master Visual Design Lawbook, human design stops being a consultancy that advises the design and becomes the constitution the design answers to. The doctrine never uses the phrase “human factors,” yet Book XI is a human-factors book in full armour, and Books V and VI encode ergonomic findings as geometry. Family II’s reading is simple: **the Lawbook’s hard numbers are percentile decisions, workload budgets, and perception thresholds, whether or not their author named them so — and they must be enforced with the field’s rigour, not the theme’s enthusiasm.**

### Book XI — the human book

Book XI — Accessibility, Performance & Resilience — organises its laws as Perception, Input, Comprehension, Resilience, Efficiency, and Fallback: a one-line map of cognitive and physical ergonomics. Its targets are the field’s targets, stated without dilution: contrast at **WCAG AA or better**; touch at **44 × 44 px minimum**; **reduced motion as a complete alternate state**, not a dimming. Its chapters — Contrast, Keyboard Navigation, Visible Focus, Reduced Motion, Text Scaling, Color-Independent Meaning, Screen Reader Semantics, Touch Targets, Cognitive Load, Information Chunking, GPU-Friendly Effects, Image Budgets, Animation Budgets, Low-Power Modes, Offline and Degraded States, Error Recovery — read as a human-design syllabus. Cognitive Load and Information Chunking carry Sweller, Miller, and Cowan into law: chunking is not a copywriting tip, it is a chapter of the book. Error Recovery carries Reason: recovery is designed, not apologised for. And the insistence that offline and degraded states are first-class surfaces is situation-awareness doctrine — the system must keep the human oriented precisely when the system itself is diminished.

### Books V and VI — the body in the geometry

Book V — Grid, Layout & Composition — legislates the perceptual field: 12 columns on desktop (16 ultrawide), outer margins 32–72 px, section gaps 32–96 px, the Three-Border Limit, and above all **Primary Workspace Calm** — the centre stays calm, intensity lives at the edges. In Gestalt terms, this is grouping and figure–ground discipline: the working content is the figure; everything else must agree to be ground. In workload terms, it is an extraneous-load budget enforced by architecture. A hall that violates calm-centre is not “rich.” It is taxing every reading act to pay for its decoration, and human design names that tax in §1’s vocabulary: split attention, search cost, fatigue.

Book VI — Component Forge — legislates the acting body: **touch target 44 × 44 px minimum, standard control height 40–48 px**, focus that survives bright and dark contexts, primary actions forged and decisive, secondary actions quiet. Note the percentile logic. WCAG 2.2 AA permits 24 px targets; the Lawbook requires 44. That gap is the difference between a legal floor written to be achievable everywhere and an ergonomic choice written for fingertips, tremor, motion, and age — the AAA region of WCAG 2.5.5, and the convergent platform guidance of §2. The Lawbook consistently chooses the human figure over the legal figure. Tove’s enforcement follows the same rule everywhere: where the standard and the body disagree, the body wins, and the standard is treated as a minimum below which we never plead convenience.

The type numbers carry the same logic: **body text 14–18 px, microtext 11–13 px for nonessential content only** (Book IV). Visual acuity and contrast sensitivity decline with age and poor light; a theme that sets essential text at the bottom of legibility for a rested young eye has designed its membership — it has chosen, in anthropometric language, a narrow percentile of vision and excluded the rest. Text scaling to **125–200%** as a coherence condition (Universal Law) is the corrective: the design must survive the user’s own accommodation, as a physical workstation must survive adjustment.

### Motion, sound, and the vestibular truth

Book X budgets motion at **80–140 ms micro, 160–220 ms standard, 260–360 ms structural, 450–800 ms ceremonial and rare**, and demands causality: every animation answers *what changed*. Human design adds the body’s reason. Motion on screens can induce discomfort in people with vestibular disorders — the reason WCAG addresses animation from interactions and why operating systems expose reduced-motion preferences. The Lawbook’s rule that reduced motion is a *complete alternate state* is therefore not politeness; it is the only implementation that respects the finding. A dimmed or slowed version of a Bifröst Sweep that still sweeps has not accommodated anyone. Likewise UI sound and haptics (Book X, Ingvild’s craft) must carry information — a distinct signal for a distinct state — because audition masks, startles, and fatigues, and a haptic buzz that means nothing trains its user to ignore the one that means something. Alarm discipline, learned in cockpits and wards, applies unaltered to a hall’s notification sounds.

### The acceptance battery as a human-design examination

Every item of the universal acceptance battery is a human-limits test in disguise. Primary task obvious within roughly **three seconds** tests perception and situation awareness under first exposure. Coherence **in grayscale** tests whether meaning survives the colour channel’s removal — for the colour-blind member, the bright room, the failing display. Usability **with glow and art disabled** tests whether hierarchy was ever structural. **Keyboard and pointer equivalents for all states** test whether the design assumed a hand that every member has. **Long labels and real content** test working memory and layout under the loads real language imposes. **Colour never the only indicator** is §1’s colour-vision data as a gate. **Compact and expanded viewports** test reach, reflow, and posture across devices. **Decorative assets never intercepting input or screen-reader order** tests whether ornament respects both the fingertip and the listening user. **Animation reducible without losing meaning** tests the vestibular accommodation. Book XIV’s removal tests — No-Rune, No-Glow, No-Art, Grayscale, Distance, Long-Content, Empty-State, Failure-State — are then exactly what a human-factors examiner would order: degrade the conditions, lengthen the content, break the system, and watch whether the human can still perceive, comprehend, and act. Mainstream practice treats these as QA. The Lawbook, correctly, treats them as the design’s proof of humanity.

### Samtal applications

In Samtal, human design is concrete. The message stream stays calm (Book VII) because reading is a sustained comprehension task whose centre must not spend workload on theatre. Presence pairs colour with label or shape because colour vision varies. Agent state is visible without diagnostics (Book VIII) because situation awareness — Endsley’s perception and comprehension — is the hall’s duty, not the member’s homework. Alt text with embedded descriptions, Volmarr’s standing law, guarantee that perception is not rationed by sense. Ornament density as a user setting (Book XIII) is adjustability — the oldest ergonomic remedy — applied to sensory load.

What Cyber-Viking practice changes, finally, is enforcement. Mainstream human factors too often ends as a report a deadline can outrank. In the Lawbook’s hall, human limits are tokenised, gated (Book XIV), and held by a named warden with a veto. That is the field’s founding lesson — Chapanis did not publish a suggestion about control shapes; the shapes changed — given institutional form.


## 4. Enforceable Rules for the Design Agent Families

The following rules bind all four families, with Family II as enforcer and Family IV as witness for the body in art and avatar work. Each rule is testable and traces to §§1–3. Where a rule gives a number, the number is the test.

1. Every design SHALL state its intended population as ranges of size, ability, age, and sensory capacity before design begins; a design with no stated population SHALL NOT proceed to review. (§1, anthropometrics; §2, judgement.)
2. Clearance SHALL be designed for the large body and reach — physical and on-screen — for the small body and the limited hand; families SHALL NOT invert these percentile directions. (§1, anthropometrics.)
3. Touch targets SHALL be at least 44 × 44 px, with visible spacing that prevents adjacent-target capture, and standard controls SHALL be 40–48 px high. The WCAG AA 24 px figure SHALL NOT be cited to justify smaller product targets. (§§2–3, Books VI and XI.)
4. Essential text SHALL be set at 14–18 px body size or larger; 11–13 px microtext SHALL carry nonessential content only and SHALL NOT present instructions, errors, values, or state. (§3, Book IV.)
5. Every layout SHALL survive text scaling to 200% and reflow without two-dimensional scrolling at narrow widths, without loss of content or function. (§2, WCAG 1.4.4, 1.4.10; §3, Universal Law.)
6. Contrast SHALL meet WCAG AA or better — 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text and for essential non-text components and focus indicators — measured in every theme state, including over imagery and glow. (§§2–3, Book XI.)
7. Colour SHALL NOT be the only means of conveying status, presence, error, selection, or any essential information; every colour signal SHALL have a text, shape, icon, pattern, or positional equivalent perceivable by screen reader and in grayscale. (§1, colour vision; §3, Books VII and XI.)
8. Working information SHALL be chunked into groups a person can hold — no unchunked codes, values, or option sets SHALL be required from memory across screens; needed context SHALL remain visible or one action away. (§1, working memory and cognitive load.)
9. No working surface SHALL place intricate ornament, animation, or high-contrast texture behind text or data; the Primary Workspace Calm SHALL be preserved, with intensity confined to shell and identity zones. (§§1, 3; Book V.)
10. Motion SHALL conform to the Lawbook bands — 80–140 ms micro, 160–220 ms standard, 260–360 ms structural, 450–800 ms ceremonial and rare — SHALL be causal, and SHALL NOT loop ambiently on working surfaces. (§3, Book X.)
11. Every animated or auto-moving surface SHALL provide a complete reduced-motion alternate state that preserves all meaning and state; dimming or slowing the same motion SHALL NOT count as accommodation. (§3, Books X–XI.)
12. UI sound and haptics SHALL each map to a specific, distinct state or event; families SHALL NOT ship ambient, decorative, or duplicate signals, and all sound and haptic signals SHALL be user-silenceable. (§1, audition and touch; §3, Book X.)
13. For every consequential action, the design SHALL name the foreseeable slip, lapse, and mistake; SHALL provide a constraint, distinction, or confirmation against the slip; visible state against the lapse; and a plain-language recovery path against all three. (§1, error taxonomy; §2, error analysis.)
14. Destructive controls SHALL be perceptually distinct from constructive controls by position, label, and form — not by colour alone — following the control-distinction principle, and SHALL be separated from high-frequency actions to prevent capture slips. (§1, history and slips.)
15. Sustained-attention surfaces — message streams, dashboards, monitoring views — SHALL present state changes by redundant, summarised signals rather than raw event floods, and SHALL keep current state perceivable and comprehensible without interrogation. (§1, vigilance and situation awareness; §3, Book VIII.)
16. Automation and agent behaviour SHALL keep the human able to perceive, comprehend, and project system state, and SHALL disclose capability and limits in human-readable form; no agent SHALL present itself in a way that invites trust beyond its disclosed capability. (§1, situation awareness and out-of-the-loop; §3, Book VIII.)
17. Every image that carries information SHALL carry a genuine alt description, embedded as data in the file per Volmarr’s standing law; decorative images SHALL be removed from screen-reader order and SHALL NOT intercept input. (§3, acceptance battery.)
18. Testing for any release SHALL include users or simulations beyond the rested, sighted, two-handed, desktop case — at minimum: keyboard-only, screen-reader, 200% zoom, reduced motion, grayscale, one-handed or small-viewport, and long-content conditions — and failures in any condition SHALL be rated by severity, not waived as edge cases. (§2, judgement; §3, Book XIV.)
19. Workload SHALL be treated as a budget: any addition of motion, sound, badges, notifications, or ambient theatre to a surface SHALL name the attention it costs and the element removed or quieted to pay for it. (§1, workload and cognitive load.)
20. No surface SHALL pass the Book XIV gate — removal tests, state tests, and Master Screen Review — while a known human-limits defect (target, contrast, scaling, motion, colour-only meaning, or memory demand) remains unresolved; Tove’s veto on such a defect SHALL stand until repair and re-test. (§3, Book XIV.)

**Honesty limits.** No studies, statistics, or citations have been invented. Population figures for colour-vision deficiency are stated approximately, as the literature states them for the populations named, and vary by ancestry. Historical episodes (wartime control coding, vigilance watchkeeping) are summarised at the level at which standard histories agree; they are teaching accounts, not archival claims. Platform guidance figures (Apple, Material) are described as convergent guidance, not standards. Any number that becomes load-bearing for a release decision must be re-verified against the current edition of its standard before the Book XIV gate — human limits as law means the law is read fresh, not remembered fondly.
